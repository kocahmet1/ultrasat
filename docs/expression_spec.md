### 3d. The four classic traps — with corpus rates

**(a) Reverse-relation distractor.** In **103 of the 133 items whose key has a natural polar
opposite (77%)** the option set contains a distractor from the opposite family. Rates by key:
CONCESSION 13/13 (100%), EMPHASIS 9/9 (100%), EXAMPLE 9/9 (100%), ADDITION 10/11 (91%), CONTRAST
22/28 (79%), RESULT 22/30 (73%), REPLACEMENT 3/5 (60%), SIMILARITY 4/7 (57%), SEQUENCE 11/21 (52%).
**Build rule: always include one reversal.**

**(b) Locally plausible for one clause, wrong for the whole logic.** Detectable in the rationales:
138/157 rationales use the formula *"'X' illogically signals that … **Instead,** …"* — the
"Instead" clause exists precisely because the distractor is defensible until you check the *whole*
relation. 144/157 rationales contain that rebuttal sentence. Canonical instance: `For instance,` in
`cf11282b` (molybdenum genuinely *is* a further case of element isolation — but it is a contrasting
case, not an illustrative one).

**(c) Two near-synonyms, neither of which can be right.** 16 items are 2-1-1; in **10 of them the
duplicated relation is *not* the key's**, so both members of the pair are simultaneously
eliminable. Verbatim pairs used this way: `accordingly,/consequently,` (00221c00), `In contrast,/
Meanwhile,` (3fd0ab63), `Conversely,/Alternatively,` (f5149550), `Similarly,/In comparison,`
(20e4ff59), `regardless,/admittedly,` (2df7b582), `in contrast,/meanwhile,` (4f2710ab),
`Nevertheless,/Even so,` (c61fb134), `In any case,/Nevertheless,` (ac8eb085), `Granted,/Regardless,`
(70c19cf6), `Ultimately limited…/Despite its creation…` (7dbcb7f4).

**(d) Additive where contrast is required.** 85% of items (134/157) carry at least one
additive/elaborative distractor (ADDITION, SIMILARITY, EXAMPLE, PARTICULAR, RESTATEMENT); 62%
(97/157) carry at least one contrastive distractor. For the 41 items keyed CONTRAST/CONCESSION/
REPLACEMENT, an additive distractor is present in 39 (95%).

## 4. What is actually being measured

**Construct (precise statement).** Given a two-clause or three-clause proposition sequence in which
the *propositional content* of clause *n* stands in exactly one discourse relation to the
accumulated content of clauses 1…*n*−1, the test-taker must (i) paraphrase both sides in their own
terms, (ii) name the relation, and (iii) select the connective whose conventional semantics encode
that relation and no other. The item is scored on discourse-relation identification, not on
connective vocabulary: 56% of Easy and Medium keys but only **34% of Hard keys** are drawn from the
common textbook inventory, so at Hard the examinee cannot succeed by matching a memorised list.

**Engineering that forces content-recovery.** Explicit connective cues are deliberately scarce in
the stimulus:

| planted cue | items | % of 157 | Easy | Medium | Hard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| digits / a numeral | 78 | 49.7% | 48% | 53% | 47% |
| a temporal ordinal (first/then/next/later/after/before) | 43 | 27.4% | 27% | 32% | 19% |
| hedges (may, might, could, suggests, appears, likely) | 38 | 24.2% | 23% | 27% | 22% |
| research vocabulary (researcher/study/team/analysis) | 36 | 22.9% | 20% | 29% | 19% |
| em dash | 30 | 19.1% | 6% | 27% | 31% |
| quotation marks | 29 | 18.5% | 11% | 25% | 22% |
| directional comparatives (more/less/higher/lower/increase/double) | 27 | 17.2% | 15% | 20% | 16% |
| semicolon | 18 | 11.5% | 3% | 14% | 25% |
| negation (not/no/never/neither/n't) | 17 | 10.8% | 6% | 14% | 16% |
| **but** | 15 | 9.6% | 11% | 5% | 16% |
| **however** (inside the stimulus) | 8 | 5.1% | 8% | 2% | 6% |
| while | 6 | 3.8% | — | — | 0% |
| **though** (subordinator) | 5 | 3.2% | 5% | 3% | 0% |
| yet | 3 | 1.9% | | | |
| because | 3 | 1.9% | | | |
| **although** | 2 | 1.3% | | | |
| **despite** | 2 | 1.3% | | | |
| **unlike / whereas** | **0** | **0%** | | | |

So: `unlike` and `whereas` never appear; `although` and `despite` appear twice each in 157 items.
The examinee cannot ride a subordinator to the answer. Where a connective *is* planted, it is
almost always in the **clause before** the blank and it is a **decoy for the wrong relation** —
e.g. `2b08f514` plants `yet … remained unchanged` (pulls toward CONCESSION; key is SEQUENCE);
`be44fea0` plants the noun "result" (pulls toward RESULT; key is REPLACEMENT).

The two cues that *are* load-bearing and are planted deliberately:
1. **Numbers moving up or down** (49.7% of items). `1c36e3e1` (180 → 2 sunspots) and PT4's Mauna Loa
   item (74,000 → 148,000 km³) both make the relation recoverable *only* by comparing quantities.
2. **A connective in the sentence *after* the blank** (`f5959727`: "Still, one sees natural
   influences…" retro-confirms that the blank must be conceding).

## 5. Stimulus anatomy (n = 157)

| measure | min | p25 | median | mean | p75 | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| stimulus words | 27 | 50 | **56** | 54.8 | 60 | 80 |

- **Sentence count:** 2 sentences 66 items (42%), 3 sentences 76 (48%), 4 sentences 12, 1 sentence 2,
  5 sentences 1. → **90% are 2 or 3 sentences.**
- **Blank position:** sentence 2 = 81 (51.6%), sentence 3 = 65 (41.4%), sentence 4 = 6, sentence 1 = 4,
  sentence 5 = 1.
- **Sentences preceding the blank:** 1 (81 items) or 2 (65 items) in 93% of cases.
- **Blank is in the FINAL sentence: 132/157 = 84%.** (When it is not, the trailing sentence usually
  carries a confirming connective — see §4.)
- **Blank is sentence-initial: 130/157 = 83%.** Exactly one blank per stimulus in 157/157.
- **What precedes the blank:** a period 130 (83%), a comma 15 (9.6%), a semicolon 9 (5.7%), a close
  quote 2, an em dash 1.
- **Surface format of the blank:** six underscores `______` in the Question Bank export, seven
  `_______` in the practice-test PDFs. It is followed by **a single space and then the next word,
  with no punctuation** (156/157; the one exception is followed by an apostrophe).
  **The blank swallows the transition *and* its comma.**
- **Options:** **628/628 end with a comma.** 1–8 words, median 1, mean 1.6. When the blank is
  sentence-initial the four options are capitalised; when it follows a comma / semicolon / dash the
  four options are lowercase. The mapping is deterministic — 0 mixed-case items.

**Template to build from:**
```
[Sentence 1: 15–30 words. Establishes claim / measurement / finding / step.]
[Optional sentence 2: 10–25 words. Adds the second term of the relation.]
______ [rest of the final sentence, 15–30 words, beginning lowercase.]

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A. Xxxxx,
B. Xxxxx,
C. Xxxxx,
D. Xxxxx,
```
The stem is **invariant**: `Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?`
(157/157, character-for-character).

  in fact, next, then, finally, previously, later, for example}; ≤1 shared content word between the
  two clauses is fine because one clause paraphrases nothing — the relation is carried by an
  explicit lexical hook (`closed the area … so experts could` → RESULT). Concession keys are almost
  banned at Easy (1/66).
- **Medium:** 55–60 words; 2–3 sentences; key may be PARTICULAR (`specifically,` — 0 Easy keys,
  6 Medium), CONSEQUENCE with a hedged finding, or CONCESSION (8/59). Add an em dash or an embedded
  quotation (27% / 25% of Medium items).
- **Hard:** 58–65 words, **never fewer than 2 or more than 3 sentences**; abstraction proxy ≥ 0.22
  long-word share; the two clauses must **share 2+ content words** so that surface similarity
  *invites* the SIMILARITY/ADDITION reading that is wrong; key drawn from outside the common
  inventory (66% of Hard keys are); punctuate with a semicolon or em dash (25% / 31%); include a
  CONCESSION or PURPOSE key in ~20% of Hard items.

`INFERRED`: the Jaccard number falls at Hard while the *raw* shared-word count rises — Hard items
are longer with more repeated key terms but proportionally more new vocabulary. Both effects push
the examinee to re-read rather than pattern-match.

## 7. Note-block anatomy (n = 143)

Fixed lead-in, verbatim and invariant (143/143):
> `While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:`

| measure | min | p25 | median | mean | p75 | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| bullets per item | 3 | 5 | **5** | 5.0 | 5 | 7 |
| words per bullet (710 bullets) | 4 | 10 | **13** | 13.5 | 16 | 48 |
| total note-block words | 27 | 57 | **69** | 66.9 | 77 | 130 |

Bullet-count distribution: **5 bullets = 99 items (69%)**, 4 = 22 (15%), 6 = 19 (13%), 3 = 2, 7 = 1.

**Form of the bullets.** They are **full declarative sentences, not fragments**: 693/710 begin with
a capital letter and **692/710 end in a period** (the remaining 17 end in a closing curly quote
because the sentence ends in a quotation, e.g. `…called "flauna."`). No bullet is a noun phrase; no
bullet uses a comma splice or a semicolon list. Subject–verb–complement, one proposition per bullet,
present or simple past tense. Pronouns freely refer back to the previous bullet's subject
(`It was invented by chemists Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch in 1910.`).

**Ordering.** Three orders occur, and they are goal-driven (`INFERRED` from reading the 143 blocks;
the pattern is consistent):
1. **General → specific / funnel** (most common). Bullet 1 names the field, object, or phenomenon;
   bullets 2–3 narrow to a specific instance; bullets 4–5 give the detail the key needs.
   `e2693197`: exhibition → artist → one painting → what the painting depicts → how the fabric is
   attached.
2. **Method chronology (study items).** Context → who studied it and why → what they did →
   what they measured → what they found. `afec1a70`: nests are flexible → team wanted to understand
   why → used lab models → analysed contact points → found stiffening under pressure.
3. **Two-column comparison (contrast/similarity goals).** Bullets alternate or block by subject:
   A-fact, A-fact, B-fact, B-fact, with the shared dimension named in bullet 1.

**Where the answer lives.** The key draws on bullets in positions 3, 4 and 5 far more than 1 and 2
(usage counts by position: 54 / 59 / 69 / 78 / 65 / 12 / 1). Bullet 1 is usually orientation the
key does *not* need.

## 9. How the four options work

**Length.** Every option is **one sentence** (559/571 single-sentence; only 12 options in the whole
bank are two sentences, and only 2 of those are keys).

| | min | p25 | median | mean | p75 | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| all options | 4 | 15 | **19** | 19.8 | 24 | 43 |
| the key | 4 | 16 | **21** | 21.3 | 26 | 43 |
| distractors | 6 | 15 | **19** | 19.3 | 23 | 36 |

The key is on average **2.0 words longer** than the mean distractor, but length is *not* a reliable
cue: key is the longest option in 46/143 (32%), middle-length in 68 (48%), shortest in 28 (20%).
Key-letter distribution is flat: A 39, B 33, C 36, D 35.

**How many notes each option uses** (≥3 shared content words and ≥30% of the bullet's content):
key draws on **mean 2.36 bullets (median 2; distribution 0:9, 1:18, 2:58, 3:35, 4:17, 5:5, 6:1)`;
distractors draw on **mean 1.98 (median 2)**. So the key **fuses about one more note than the
average distractor** — synthesis, not retrieval. Items whose goal is G1/G2 (difference/similarity)
require exactly 2 notes, one per subject; G4/G10 (study overview, process) require 3–4.

**Distractor taxonomy (425 distractor rationales, machine-tagged, multi-label):**

| n | % of distractors | type |
|---|---|---|
| 332 | **78.1%** | **(a) Accurate and drawn from the notes, but does not accomplish the stated goal.** Rationale marker: *"This choice doesn't <goal verb>…"* / *"While the sentence <does X>, it doesn't <goal>."* |
| 179 | **42.1%** | **(c) Uses the notes but the wrong ones / a different fact.** Rationale marker: the segment opens *"The sentence describes / presents / identifies / only provides …"* then pivots. Overlaps heavily with (a). |
| 27 | **6.4%** | **(b) Distorts or invents a fact.** Markers: *"mischaracterizes information from the notes"*, *"the notes don't indicate…"*, *"incorrectly suggests"*. |
| 3 | 0.7% | **(d) Too vague / general to accomplish the goal.** Rare; usually merged into (a) as *"only states a general fact"*. |

**The single most important design fact: 94% of distractors are factually true to the notes.**
Distortion is a minority device (6.4%). The discrimination is entirely rhetorical — right content,
wrong job.

**Four verbatim option sets with the official rationale reasoning:**

**(1) `afec1a70` — Medium — key A — 5 bullets / 75 note-words — goal type G4**
> • As engineered structures, many bird nests are uniquely flexible yet cohesive.
> • A research team led by Yashraj Bhosale wanted to better understand the mechanics behind these structural properties.
> • Bhosale's team used laboratory models that simulated the arrangement of flexible sticks into nest-like structures.
> • The researchers analyzed the points where sticks touched one another.
> • When pressure was applied to the model nests, the number of contact points between the sticks increased, making the structures stiffer.

  study. It describes how the study worked, but not why it was done."* → type (a).

- D (14 w) — the *premise* re-attributed. *"This choice doesn't present the primary aim of the
  research study."* → (a)/(c).

  frame for every G4 item.

**(2) `4c43bf61` — Medium — key B — goal type G7 (advantage)**
> The student wants to explain an advantage of the ICAA's archive being digital.

**(3) `e2693197` — Easy — key A — goal type G10/G6**

## 11. Within-form repetition constraints

**Corpus caveat.** The practice-test files in this corpus are the **linear paper forms**, whose RW
modules hold **33 questions** (33 `D)` lines per module file, question numbering running past 27).
The adaptive Bluebook module is 27. Counts below are per 33-item module; the 27-item equivalents
are scaled in the last column.

| module | Transitions | Rhet. Synthesis | Expression of Ideas total | % of 33 | scaled to 27 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PT4 Module 1 | 4 | 3 | 7 | 21% | ~6 |
| PT4 Module 2 | 2 | 5 | 7 | 21% | ~6 |
| PT5 Module 1 | 4–5 | 3 | 7–8 | 21–24% | ~6 |
| PT5 Module 2 | 4 | 4 | 8 | 24% | ~6–7 |
| PT2 (whole 66-item form) | 4 | 7–8 | 11–12 | 17–18% | ~9–10 / 54 |
| PT3 (whole 66-item form) | 5 | 8 | 13 | 20% | ~11 / 54 |

(One PT5 M1 "logical transition" hit is a two-column extraction bleed, hence the 4–5 range.)

**Working numbers for a new 54-item (2 × 27) form:** **4–6 Transitions and 6–8 Rhetorical Synthesis,
10–12 Expression of Ideas items total (≈20%)**; per 27-item module **2–3 Transitions + 3–4 Rhetorical
Synthesis**. Expression of Ideas items cluster at the **end of each module** — in PT4 Module 1 the
four Transitions items are consecutive (Q27, Q28, Q29, Q30) and the RS items follow them.

**Does the same logical relation repeat inside one module?**
PT4 Module 1's four Transitions items are, in order:
1. Conyers/MLK holiday bill → key `Finally,` [SEQUENCE]
2. Mauna Loa vs. Pūhāhonu volumes → key `However,` [CONTRAST]
3. Coleridge-Taylor → key `In addition,` [ADDITION] (= Question Bank item `660d50dc`, key confirmed)
4. pašrūtum stew → key `Therefore,` [RESULT]
→ **four distinct relations, no repeat.** `INFERRED` (only item 3 has a bank-confirmed key; 1, 2, 4
are read off the text).
PT5 Module 1 (`INFERRED`): reduction firing → `As a result,` [RESULT]; turtle shell → `However,`
[CONTRAST]; Jelly Roll Morton → `though,` [CONCESSION] (= bank item `ff1a2e5e`, confirmed);
Duverger → `Conversely,` [CONTRAST]. → **CONTRAST would repeat once.**
**Rule:** treat relation repetition inside a module as *allowed but rare*; never repeat the same
transition **string**. Across a 54-item form, the four highest-frequency relations
(RESULT, CONTRAST, SEQUENCE, CONCESSION) should each appear once, with 1–2 slots for the long tail.

**Does the same goal-type repeat?** **Yes — routinely, and even verbatim.**
- PT4 Module 1 (3 RS): *describe Unwoven Light to an audience unfamiliar with Soo Sunny Park* /
  *emphasize a difference between baking soda and baking powder* / *present Tan's research to an
  audience unfamiliar with Angkor Wat* → **G5 twice in one module.**
- PT4 Module 2 (5 RS): *present the influence theory to an audience unfamiliar with the
  Haudenosaunee Confederacy* / *introduce Cathryn Halverson's book to an audience already familiar
  with the Atlantic Monthly* / *explain an advantage of the Hanke-Henry calendar* / *emphasize how
  hot the Sun is relative to nearby stars* / *emphasize a similarity between the two ways a
  magnificent frigatebird acquires food* → **G5 twice again** (once "unfamiliar", once "already
  familiar" — the paired variant).
- PT5 Module 1 (3 RS): *specify how the salt enables energy storage* / *identify the real author of
  Adam Bede* / *indicate the California red-legged frog's FWS classification category* → **G9 twice.**
- PT3 (whole form, 8 RS): `The student wants to emphasize the aim of the research study.` appears
  **twice, verbatim identical**, plus *emphasize the study's methodology* — three study-goal items
  in one form.
**Rule: goal *types* may repeat within a module (and do, ~1 pair per 3–5 RS items), but the two
members of a repeated pair take different sub-frames** (unfamiliar vs. already-familiar audience;
specify-a-number vs. identify-a-name).

## 12. Rationale style

### 12a. Transitions — two templates

**Template A "logically signals" — 134/157 items (85%)** (Easy 56, Medium 51, Hard 27).
`"logically signals"` occurs in 138/157; `"illogically signals"` in 138/157; the `". Instead, …"`
rebuttal sentence in 144/157; an em-dash appositive gloss inside the key sentence in 101/157.
Rationale length: **min 117, median 176, mean 180, max 271 words.**

```
Choice <K> is the best answer. "<Transition>" logically signals that <the information in this
sentence> — <em-dash appositive restating the blank-sentence content in the rationale's own
words> — <RELATION VERB PHRASE> <restatement of the preceding sentence's content>.
Choice <X> is incorrect because "<transition>" illogically signals that <the relation that
transition would assert, applied to this content>. Instead, <one-clause restatement of the true
relation>.
[repeat for the remaining two distractors, reusing the same "Instead," clause almost verbatim]
```
Relation verb phrases used, verbatim: *"contrasts with"*, *"is a result of"* / *"occurred as a
consequence or result of"*, *"is similar to"*, *"exemplifies"*, *"adds to"*, *"provides specific,
precise details elaborating on"*, *"is merely restating or rephrasing"*, *"is part of a
chronological sequence of events"*, *"is the next step in"*, *"acknowledges an obvious limitation
of, or qualification to"*, *"offers additional emphasis in support of"*, *"is a means of
accomplishing a goal established in"*, *"clarifies the terms of"*, *"is true despite"*.

**Template B "plain-language" — 23/157 (15%)** (Easy 10, Medium 8, Hard 5): no `signals`
vocabulary, `Choice X is incorrect.` with a period, and each distractor is dismissed by naming the
*category* of transition:
> *"Choice A is incorrect. This choice uses a transition that indicates a restatement of the same
> idea, which doesn't make sense here. This sentence doesn't restate the first example — it
> describes a totally different example."*

**Three verbatim key-sentences (Template A):**
1. `cf11282b` — *"Choice A is the best answer. "By contrast" logically signals that the information
   in this sentence — that isolating molybdenum was a relatively long process — contrasts with the
   previous information that isolating selenium was a short process."*
2. `f5959727` — *"Choice C is the best answer. "Of course" logically signals that the information in
   this sentence — that "the wavy staircase and ornate floral tilework" of the Modernista hospital
   couldn't actually grow in a forest — acknowledges an obvious limitation of, or qualification to,
   the previous claim that Modernista architects championed nature in their designs."*
3. `e3edc138` — *"Choice D is the best answer. "That is" logically signals that this sentence
   clarifies the terms of the scientific debate introduced in the previous sentence by explaining
   the difference between dispersalists and vicariancists."*

**Three verbatim distractor dismissals:**
1. *"Choice B is incorrect because "thus" illogically signals that the information in this sentence
   is a result of the previous information about selenium's short isolation process. Instead, the
   sentence presents contrasting information about molybdenum's longer isolation process."*
2. *"Choice C is incorrect because "for example" illogically signals that the discovery about jorō
   spiders' gentleness exemplifies the initial hypothesis about their aggressive behavior. Instead,
   the sentence presents information that contradicts the initial hypothesis."*
3. *"Choice A is incorrect because "lastly" illogically signals that this sentence presents the last
   of a series of points or reasons. Instead, it describes a later event occurring in a
   chronological sequence of events."*

### 12b. Rhetorical Synthesis — one template

`"Choice X is incorrect."` (period form) in **139/143**; `"is incorrect because"` in only 11.
The key sentence opens `Choice K is the best answer. The sentence …` in 107/143 and

about **35% shorter than a Transitions rationale**.

```
Choice <K> is the best answer. The sentence <goal verb, repeated verbatim from the goal
sentence>, <gerund clause naming the specific note content that discharges the goal>.
Choice <X> is incorrect. The sentence <accurate description of what that option does>; it
doesn't <goal clause, repeated verbatim>.
[×3]
```

2. `afec1a70` — *"Choice A is the best answer. It describes the reason Bhosale's team wanted to

## Appendix — Build checklist

**Transitions item**
1. Pick a relation from the §2 distribution (weight RESULT 19%, CONTRAST 18%, SEQUENCE 13%,
   CONCESSION 8%, ADDITION 7%, EMPHASIS 6%, EXAMPLE 6%, …).
2. Write 2–3 sentences, 50–60 words total, on a named person / dated event / measured finding.
3. Put the blank at the head of the **final** sentence (84%), preceded by a period (83%);
   6–7 underscores, one space, then a **lowercase** continuation.
4. Key = a transition from that relation. At Hard, choose one *outside* the common list.
5. Distractors: one polar reversal (77% of items have one), one additive/elaborative
   (85% have one), one from a third family. Four distinct relations (89.8%); if you use a repeated
   relation, make both members **distractors** (10 of the 16 repeats).
6. Every option ends with a comma; all four share capitalisation.
7. Verify no `unlike`/`whereas`, ≤1 `but`, and that the answer survives deleting every explicit
   connective from the stimulus.

**Rhetorical Synthesis item**
1. Fixed lead-in; **5 bullets** (69%); each a complete sentence ending in a period;
   65–75 total words at Medium.
2. Order: orientation → narrowing → the two or three facts the key must fuse (put them at
   positions 3–5).
3. Goal sentence: `The student wants to <goal>.` 13–14 words, from the §8 list; then the invariant
   stem.
4. Key: one sentence, 19–24 words, fusing **2 (Medium) or 3–4 (Hard)** bullets.
5. Distractors: three single sentences, 15–23 words, **all factually true to the notes**, each
   using different bullets; each fails the goal in a different way (wrong facet of the study, wrong
   bullet pair, context instead of content). Reserve fact-distortion for ≤1 in 4 items.
6. Rationale: repeat the goal clause verbatim in all four segments.
