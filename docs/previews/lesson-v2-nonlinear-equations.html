Nonlinear Equations

A nonlinear equation contains a squared variable (or a variable under a root), so it can have $0$, $1$, or $2$ real solutions instead of always one. The centerpiece is the quadratic equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, and you have three tools for it—plus one detector that counts solutions without solving at all.

ToolUse it when…
Factoring + zero productthe quadratic factors nicely (integer roots)
Square-root methodthere's a perfect square but no lone $x$-term: $(x - 3)^2 = 25$
Quadratic formulaalways works—your fallback when factoring isn't obvious
Discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$the question asks how many solutions, not what they are

Strategies

  • Get one side to $0$ first. Factoring and the formula both need the form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.
  • Zero product: if a product equals $0$, some factor equals $0$—that's what makes factoring solve equations.
  • Taking a square root gives two answers: $\pm$. Forgetting the negative root is the top error in this skill.
  • Never divide both sides by the variable—you'll silently delete the $x = 0$ solution.
  • Squared both sides? Check every answer in the original equation—squaring can create extraneous solutions.
  • Systems with a parabola: substitute, solve the resulting quadratic; solutions are the intersection points.
Multiple choice bailout: solutions of an equation can always be tested. Plugging the choices into the equation is never wrong—and often faster.
Advanced MathNonlinear Equations: Solve by Factoring

Solve by Factoring

Rule: Zero product property: if $A \cdot B = 0$, then $A = 0$ or $B = 0$. Factor the quadratic, set each factor to zero, and each gives a solution.

Example

What are the solutions of $x^2 + 2x - 15 = 0$? Look for two numbers that multiply to $-15$ and add to $+2$: they are $+5$ and $-3$.

$$x^2 + 2x - 15 = 0$$
Given equation — one side is already $0$
$$(x + 5)(x - 3) = 0$$
Factor: $5 \times (-3) = -15$ and $5 + (-3) = 2$
$$x = -5 \quad \text{or} \quad x = 3$$
Set each factor equal to $0$

Check one of them: $(-5)^2 + 2(-5) - 15 = 25 - 10 - 15 = 0$ ✓

Move Everything to One Side First

Example

What are the solutions of $x^2 = 5x + 24$?

$$x^2 - 5x - 24 = 0$$
Subtract $5x$ and $24$ — get $0$ on one side before factoring
$$(x - 8)(x + 3) = 0$$
$-8 \times 3 = -24$ and $-8 + 3 = -5$
$$x = 8 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -3$$
Zero product

The Forbidden Move

When both sides share a factor of $x$, dividing it away feels natural—and quietly destroys a solution:

  • $x^2 = 6x \;\Rightarrow\; x = 6$
    Divided both sides by $x$ — the solution $x = 0$ is gone. (Test it: $0^2 = 6(0)$ is true!)
  • $x^2 - 6x = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x(x - 6) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 0 \;\text{or}\; x = 6$
    Factor instead of dividing — both solutions survive.
Dividing by a variable is dividing by something that might be zero. Factor it out instead—every time.
Advanced MathNonlinear Equations: Square Roots & the Quadratic Formula

Square Roots & the Quadratic Formula

The Square-Root Method

When the equation is a perfect square equal to a number—no lone $x$-term—skip factoring entirely: take the square root of both sides, and keep both signs.

Example

What are the solutions of $(x - 3)^2 = 25$?

$$x - 3 = \pm 5$$
Square root of both sides — a square of $25$ can come from $5$ or $-5$
$$x = 3 + 5 \quad \text{or} \quad x = 3 - 5$$
Add $3$ to both sides, once for each sign
$$x = 8 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -2$$
Two solutions
The $\pm$ is not decoration. Answering only $x = 8$ throws away half the solution set—and the discarded half is usually among the wrong answers waiting for you.

The Quadratic Formula

When factoring isn't obvious, the formula solves any quadratic in standard form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$:

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$
Example

What are the solutions of $3x^2 - 5x - 2 = 0$? Here $a = 3$, $b = -5$, $c = -2$:

$$x = \frac{-(-5) \pm \sqrt{(-5)^2 - 4(3)(-2)}}{2(3)}$$
Plug in $a$, $b$, $c$ — parentheses around every negative
$$x = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{25 + 24}}{6} = \frac{5 \pm 7}{6}$$
$\sqrt{49} = 7$
$$x = 2 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -\tfrac{1}{3}$$
$\frac{12}{6}$ and $\frac{-2}{6}$

(This one also factors—$(3x + 1)(x - 2) = 0$—but the formula doesn't care whether you spotted that.)

Formula sign traps: $-b$ when $b$ is negative becomes positive, and $-4ac$ with a negative $c$ becomes addition under the root. Write the substitution line fully before simplifying.
Advanced MathNonlinear Equations: How Many Solutions? The Discriminant

How Many Solutions? The Discriminant

Often the SAT doesn't want the solutions—just how many there are. The answer lives entirely under the square root of the quadratic formula:

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{\,\textcolor{#0f6cbd}{b^2 - 4ac}\,}}{2a}$$
b² − 4ac — the discriminant: the only part that decides the number of real solutions

A positive number has two square roots, zero has one, and a negative number has none (among real numbers). So:

$b^2 - 4ac > 0$ — two
$b^2 - 4ac = 0$ — one
$b^2 - 4ac < 0$ — zero
Example

How many distinct real solutions does the equation $3x^2 - 6x + 5 = 0$ have?

  1. A)Zero
  2. B)Exactly one
  3. C)Exactly two
  4. D)Infinitely many
$$b^2 - 4ac$$
Discriminant, with $a = 3$, $b = -6$, $c = 5$
$$(-6)^2 - 4(3)(5) = 36 - 60$$
Substitute
$$-24 < 0$$
Negative — no real solutions
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: A negative discriminant means the square root has no real value—the parabola never touches the x-axis.
  • Choice B: Exactly one requires the discriminant to equal exactly $0$; $-24 \neq 0$.
  • Choice C: Two solutions needs a positive discriminant. If you got $+96$, you added $4ac$ instead of subtracting.
  • Choice D: A quadratic equation can't hold for every $x$—infinitely many solutions is a linear identity situation.
Example

In the equation $x^2 + 10x + k = 0$, $k$ is a constant. If the equation has exactly one real solution, what is the value of $k$?

  1. A)$25$
  2. B)$-25$
  3. C)$5$
  4. D)$50$
$$b^2 - 4ac = 0$$
Exactly one solution ⇔ discriminant equals $0$
$$(10)^2 - 4(1)k = 0$$
Substitute $a = 1$, $b = 10$, $c = k$
$$k = 25$$
$100 = 4k$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: $100 - 4(25) = 0$. ✓ (Fittingly, $x^2 + 10x + 25 = (x + 5)^2$ — a perfect square touching at $x = -5$.)
  • Choice B: $k = -25$ gives a discriminant of $200 > 0$ — two solutions, not one.
  • Choice C: $5$ is $\sqrt{25}$ — one simplification too many. The constant itself must be $25$.
  • Choice D: $50$ halves the $100$ instead of dividing by $4$.
Translate first, compute second: “two distinct real solutions” ⇔ $b^2 - 4ac > 0$; “exactly one” ⇔ $= 0$; “no real solutions” ⇔ $< 0$. Then it's arithmetic.
Advanced MathNonlinear Equations: Systems & Extraneous Solutions

Systems & Extraneous Solutions

A Line Meets a Parabola

For a system pairing a linear and a quadratic equation, substitute one into the other. The resulting quadratic's solutions are the $x$-coordinates of the intersection points—so there can be $2$, $1$ (tangent), or $0$ of them.

Example
$$\begin{cases} y = x^2 - 2x - 3 \\ y = x + 1 \end{cases}$$
$$x^2 - 2x - 3 = x + 1$$
Both equal $y$ — set them equal
$$x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0$$
Move everything to one side
$$(x - 4)(x + 1) = 0$$
Factor: $-4 \times 1 = -4$, $-4 + 1 = -3$
$$x = 4 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -1$$
Then $y = x + 1$ gives the points $(4, 5)$ and $(-1, 0)$

Extraneous Solutions

Equations with a square root get solved by squaring both sides—but squaring is a one-way door: it can manufacture solutions the original equation never had. Always test your answers in the original.

Example

What is the solution set of $\sqrt{x + 7} = x - 5$?

$$x + 7 = (x - 5)^2$$
Square both sides
$$x + 7 = x^2 - 10x + 25$$
Expand the square
$$x^2 - 11x + 18 = 0$$
Move everything to one side
$$(x - 2)(x - 9) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 2 \;\text{or}\; x = 9$$
Factor — now check both in the original
  • $x = 9$: $\sqrt{9 + 7} = \sqrt{16} = 4$ and $9 - 5 = 4$
    Both sides agree — a genuine solution.
  • $x = 2$: $\sqrt{2 + 7} = 3$ but $2 - 5 = -3$
    $3 \neq -3$: extraneous — created by squaring, not a solution of the original. The solution set is $\{9\}$ only.
A square root symbol outputs the nonnegative root, so $\sqrt{\text{anything}}$ can never equal a negative number. That's exactly the check $x = 2$ failed.
Advanced MathNonlinear Equations: Practice

Practice

Choose the tool before touching the algebra: factor, square-root method, formula, or discriminant. And keep the two safety rules live—both roots of a square, and check after squaring.

Practice
  1. 1.

    What is the positive solution of the equation $x^2 - 3x - 28 = 0$?

    1. A)$4$
    2. B)$7$
    3. C)$14$
    4. D)$28$

    The correct answer is B. Two numbers that multiply to $-28$ and add to $-3$: $-7$ and $+4$.

    $$(x - 7)(x + 4) = 0$$
    Factor
    $$x = 7 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -4$$
    Zero product — the positive one is $7$
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $4$ reads the factor $(x + 4)$ without flipping the sign—that factor's solution is $-4$.
    • Choice B: Check: $49 - 21 - 28 = 0$. ✓
    • Choice C: $14$ is $7 \times 2$—no step in the solution produces it. Distractors don't have to be clever.
    • Choice D: $28$ is the constant term, not a solution: $784 - 84 - 28 \neq 0$.
  2. 2.

    If $(x + 2)^2 = 49$, what is the sum of the solutions of the equation?

    1. A)$-4$
    2. B)$-2$
    3. C)$5$
    4. D)$14$

    The correct answer is A. Square-root method, keeping both signs:

    $$x + 2 = \pm 7$$
    Square root of both sides
    $$x = 5 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -9$$
    $-2 + 7$ and $-2 - 7$
    $$5 + (-9) = -4$$
    Sum of the solutions
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Both roots kept, then added. ✓
    • Choice B: $-2$ is the midpoint of the solutions (the axis of symmetry), not their sum.
    • Choice C: $5$ is what's left when the $\pm$ becomes just $+$: one solution, not the sum of two.
    • Choice D: $14$ adds $7 + 7$—the two square roots of $49$, not the two values of $x$.
  3. 3.

    How many distinct real solutions does the equation $2x^2 - 7x + 9 = 0$ have?

    1. A)Zero
    2. B)Exactly one
    3. C)Exactly two
    4. D)Cannot be determined

    The correct answer is A. No solving needed—just the discriminant with $a = 2$, $b = -7$, $c = 9$:

    $$(-7)^2 - 4(2)(9)$$
    $b^2 - 4ac$
    $$49 - 72 = -23$$
    Negative — the parabola never reaches the x-axis
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $-23 < 0$: zero real solutions. ✓
    • Choice B: Exactly one needs the discriminant to be exactly $0$.
    • Choice C: $49 + 72 = 121$ comes from adding $4ac$; the formula subtracts it.
    • Choice D: The discriminant always determines the count—nothing is ambiguous here.
  4. 4.
    $$\begin{cases} y = x^2 \\ y = 2x + 8 \end{cases}$$

    The graphs of the equations in the system above intersect at two points. If $(a, b)$ is the intersection point with $a > 0$, what is the value of $b$?

    1. A)$4$
    2. B)$8$
    3. C)$16$
    4. D)$-2$

    The correct answer is C. Substitute and solve:

    $$x^2 = 2x + 8$$
    Set the two expressions for $y$ equal
    $$x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (x - 4)(x + 2) = 0$$
    Standard form, then factor
    $$x = 4 \;\text{or}\; x = -2$$
    The positive one is $a = 4$
    $$b = 4^2 = 16$$
    Intersection point $(4, 16)$ — check the line: $2(4) + 8 = 16$ ✓
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $4$ is $a$ itself—and also the $y$-value of the other intersection $(-2, 4)$. Either way, not what was asked.
    • Choice B: $8$ is the line's y-intercept, a number lifted from the problem, not from the solution.
    • Choice C: $(4, 16)$ satisfies both equations. ✓
    • Choice D: $-2$ is the $x$-coordinate of the other intersection—the one the condition $a > 0$ rules out.

Things to Remember

  • Get $= 0$ first, then factor and apply the zero product property. Never divide both sides by $x$—factor it out so $x = 0$ survives.
  • Square-root method: $(x - h)^2 = n$ gives $x = h \pm \sqrt{n}$ — two answers.
  • The quadratic formula always works: $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$. Substitute negatives in parentheses.
  • The discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ counts solutions: positive → two, zero → one, negative → none. Translate the words into the right comparison before computing.
  • Line + parabola: substitute, solve the quadratic; solutions are intersection points ($2$, $1$, or $0$ of them).
  • After squaring both sides, test every answer in the original equation—$\sqrt{\ }$ can't output a negative, and extraneous roots must be discarded.
