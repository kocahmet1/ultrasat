Inference from Statistics

You can't survey every resident or weigh every apple—so statisticians measure a random sample and use its result (the sample statistic) to estimate the truth about the whole population. This skill has almost no computation; it tests whether you know what a sample can and cannot tell you.

Random sample→Sample statistic→Population estimate ± margin of error

Strategies

  • Random selection is the license to generalize. No random sample, no population conclusion.
  • Scale up: estimated population count $=$ sample proportion $\times$ population size.
  • Margin of error builds a plausible interval for the population value: estimate $\pm$ MOE. It's about sampling variation, not mistakes.
  • Bigger random sample → smaller margin of error. Precision comes from size, not enthusiasm.
  • Conclusions reach only the population actually sampled—visitors of this aquarium, subscribers of this magazine.
Correct answers in this skill are humble: “plausible,” “estimated,” “about,” and they name the sampled population precisely. Choices that say “exactly,” “all,” “every,” or leap to a bigger population are the traps.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisInference from Statistics: From Sample to Population

From Sample to Population

Rule: If the sample is selected at random, its proportion is the best estimate of the population's proportion—so multiply it by the population size to estimate a count.

Example

A random sample of $150$ students is selected from a school of $1{,}800$ students, and $42$ of the sampled students ride a bike to school. Based on the sample, approximately how many students in the school ride a bike?

$$\frac{42}{150} = 0.28$$
Sample proportion — $28\%$ of the sample rides
$$0.28 \times 1{,}800 = 504$$
Apply the proportion to the whole school

The estimate is about $504$ students—“about,” because a different random sample of $150$ would give a slightly different proportion.

Example

A city has $6{,}000$ residents. In a random sample of $200$ residents, $55$ support building a new park. Based on the sample, about how many of the city's residents support the park?

  1. A)$1{,}650$
  2. B)$55$
  3. C)$275$
  4. D)$3{,}300$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: $\frac{55}{200} = 0.275$, and $0.275 \times 6{,}000 = 1{,}650$. The population estimate keeps the sample's proportion.
  • Choice B: $55$ is the raw sample count—never scaled up to the $6{,}000$ residents.
  • Choice C: $275$ misplaces a decimal: $27.5\%$ became $275$ people instead of $27.5\%$ of $6{,}000$.
  • Choice D: $3{,}300$ applies $55\%$—reading the count $55$ as a percent. The sample proportion is $55$ out of $200$.
Proportion check: $55$ of $200$ is a bit over a quarter, so the answer should be a bit over a quarter of $6{,}000$. Estimates that break the sample's proportion are wrong at a glance.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisInference from Statistics: Margin of Error

Margin of Error

Run the survey again with a new random sample and you'd get a slightly different number—sampling comes with wiggle. The margin of error (MOE) quantifies that wiggle by turning the estimate into a plausible interval for the population value:

Example

A poll of randomly selected voters finds $62\%$ support for a measure, with a margin of error of $4\%$:

$$62\% - 4\% \;\longrightarrow\; 62\% + 4\%$$
Plausible values for the population percentage: $58\%$ to $66\%$
  • “It is plausible that between $58\%$ and $66\%$ of all voters support the measure.”
    An interval, about the population, stated with appropriate uncertainty.
  • “Exactly $62\%$ of all voters support the measure.”
    The sample can't deliver exactness—$62\%$ is the estimate, not the truth.
  • “The pollsters made up to $4\%$ worth of mistakes.”
    MOE measures sampling variation, not errors. A flawless poll still has a margin of error.

What Controls the Margin of Error

Rule: A larger random sample produces a smaller margin of error—more data, tighter interval. (Not more careful measuring, not a more enthusiastic surveyor: size.)

Example

Researchers randomly select $50$ apples from an orchard's harvest. The sample's mean weight is $172$ grams, with an associated margin of error of $6$ grams. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. A)It is plausible that the mean weight of all apples in the harvest is between 166 and 178 grams
  2. B)Every apple in the harvest weighs between 166 and 178 grams
  3. C)The mean weight of all apples in the harvest is exactly 172 grams
  4. D)The researchers' scale was off by as much as 6 grams
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Estimate $\pm$ MOE bounds the plausible population mean: $172 \pm 6$. Interval, population, hedged—all three boxes checked.
  • Choice B: The interval bounds the mean, not individual apples—plenty of single apples fall outside $166$–$178$.
  • Choice C: “Exactly” claims certainty a sample can't provide; $172$ is the estimate.
  • Choice D: MOE is about sample-to-sample variation, not equipment error.
The margin of error belongs to the population estimate—never to individuals, and never to “mistakes.” Those two misreadings are the wrong answers, every time this is tested.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisInference from Statistics: How Far Do Conclusions Reach?

How Far Do Conclusions Reach?

A sample speaks only for the population it was drawn from. Two questions decide what a study supports: was the sample random, and from which group was it selected?

How the sample was chosenConclusions can reach…
Randomly from all students at a schoolall students at that school
Randomly from one grade at the schoolthat grade only
Volunteers responding to an online postno wider population — self-selection invites bias
Example

A researcher selects $300$ subscribers of a cooking magazine at random and finds that $80\%$ of those selected enjoy baking. Which group does this result best support a conclusion about?

  1. A)All subscribers of the cooking magazine
  2. B)All adults in the country
  3. C)All people who cook
  4. D)The 300 subscribers who were surveyed, but no one else
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: The sample was drawn at random from the subscribers, so it generalizes to exactly that population—about $80\%$ of subscribers plausibly enjoy baking.
  • Choice B: No adult outside the subscriber list ever had a chance to be sampled—people who subscribe to a cooking magazine are hardly typical of all adults.
  • Choice C: Same overreach with different packaging: cooks who don't subscribe were never in the pool.
  • Choice D: Too narrow: random selection is precisely what licenses extending the result beyond the $300$ to the full subscriber population.
Bias beats size: a huge sample of volunteers is still just volunteers. Randomness—not sample size—is what earns the right to generalize; size only sharpens the margin of error afterward.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisInference from Statistics: Practice

Practice

Three checks per question: Is the answer scaled correctly? Does it hedge (“plausible,” “about”)? Does it stay inside the sampled population?

Practice
  1. 1.

    A supermarket selects a random sample of $120$ of the $2{,}400$ shoppers who visited on Saturday; $45$ of the sampled shoppers bought produce. Based on the sample, about how many of Saturday's shoppers bought produce?

    1. A)$900$
    2. B)$45$
    3. C)$375$
    4. D)$1{,}080$

    The correct answer is A.

    $$\frac{45}{120} = 0.375$$
    Sample proportion: $37.5\%$
    $$0.375 \times 2{,}400 = 900$$
    Scaled to all of Saturday's shoppers
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: A bit over a third of the sample bought produce, and $900$ is a bit over a third of $2{,}400$. ✓
    • Choice B: $45$ is the sample count, never scaled up.
    • Choice C: $375$ turns the proportion $0.375$ directly into a count—dropping the $\times 2{,}400$.
    • Choice D: $1{,}080$ applies $45\%$—reading the count $45$ as a percent.
  2. 2.

    A poll of randomly selected likely voters estimates that $48\%$ support Candidate M, with a margin of error of $3\%$. Which of the following is the best interpretation of these results?

    1. A)It is plausible that between 45% and 51% of likely voters support Candidate M
    2. B)Exactly 48% of likely voters support Candidate M
    3. C)The percentage of likely voters who support Candidate M cannot be less than 45% or greater than 51%
    4. D)3% of the voters surveyed had not decided which candidate to support

    The correct answer is A. Estimate $\pm$ MOE gives the plausible interval $45\%$–$51\%$ for the population percentage—stated with the uncertainty a sample deserves.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Interval ✓ population ✓ “plausible” ✓.
    • Choice B: “Exactly” overstates: $48\%$ describes the sample; the population value is estimated, not known.
    • Choice C: “Cannot be” turns a plausible range into an impossible-to-violate guarantee—margins of error don't promise that.
    • Choice D: The margin of error says nothing about undecided voters—it measures sampling variation.
  3. 3.

    Researchers estimate a population mean from a random sample, with an associated margin of error. Which change would most likely make the margin of error smaller?

    1. A)Selecting a larger random sample
    2. B)Selecting a smaller random sample
    3. C)Replacing random selection with volunteers, but surveying twice as many
    4. D)Reporting the estimate rounded to the nearest whole number

    The correct answer is A. More randomly selected data means sample results vary less from sample to sample—a tighter plausible interval.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Sample size is the lever that controls the margin of error.
    • Choice B: Less data → more sample-to-sample wiggle → a larger margin of error.
    • Choice C: Volunteers introduce bias that no sample size can fix—doubling a flawed sample doubles the flaw's reach, not the precision.
    • Choice D: Rounding changes how the number is written, not how uncertain it is.
  4. 4.

    A random sample of visitors to a city aquarium had a mean visit length of $95$ minutes, with a margin of error of $10$ minutes. Which conclusion is best supported by these results?

    1. A)It is plausible that the mean visit length for all visitors to this aquarium is between 85 and 105 minutes
    2. B)It is plausible that the mean visit length at all aquariums is between 85 and 105 minutes
    3. C)Every visitor to this aquarium stays between 85 and 105 minutes
    4. D)Most visitors to this aquarium stay exactly 95 minutes

    The correct answer is A. Interval for the mean, hedged with “plausible,” restricted to the population sampled—visitors of this one aquarium.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $95 \pm 10$, about this aquarium's visitors. All three checks pass.
    • Choice B: Other aquariums were never sampled—the conclusion can't travel there.
    • Choice C: The interval bounds the mean, not individual visits—someone's quick 20-minute walkthrough doesn't violate it.
    • Choice D: “Exactly $95$” misreads an estimate as a fact about individuals—two traps in one.

Things to Remember

  • A random sample's statistic is the best estimate of the population's value—randomness is the license to generalize.
  • Estimated population count $=$ sample proportion $\times$ population size. Check that the answer keeps the sample's proportion.
  • Margin of error → plausible interval for the population value: estimate $\pm$ MOE. It reflects sampling variation—not mistakes, and not individuals.
  • Larger random sample → smaller margin of error. Bias can't be fixed by size.
  • Conclusions reach exactly as far as the sampled population—no further.
  • Pick answers that say “plausible,” “about,” “estimated”; reject “exactly,” “every,” “cannot be,” and population upgrades.
