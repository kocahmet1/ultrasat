One-Variable Data

One-variable data is a list of values of a single quantity—test scores, books read, salaries. The SAT asks two things about such a list: where is its center, and how wide is its spread? Five measures cover everything:

MeasureWhat it tells youHow to find it
Meanthe average — the balance point$\dfrac{\text{sum of values}}{\text{number of values}}$
Medianthe middle valuesort, take the middle (average the two middles if the count is even)
Modethe most frequent valuecount repeats
Rangetotal width of the datamaximum $-$ minimum
Standard deviationtypical distance from the meannever computed on the SAT — only compared: more spread out = larger

Strategies

  • Sort before finding the median—and check whether the count is odd or even.
  • Work the mean in both directions: $\text{sum} = \text{mean} \times \text{count}$ unlocks every missing-value question.
  • Outliers drag the mean and barely move the median. When a question contrasts the two, an outlier is the reason.
  • Standard deviation by eye: clustered near the mean → small; stretched out → large. No formula, ever.
  • Read displays carefully: in dot plots and histograms, height is a count of data points, not a data value.
Almost every hard question in this skill is one of two games: “find the missing value from the mean” (use sum $=$ mean $\times$ count) or “mean vs. median” (find the outlier). Name the game first.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisOne-Variable Data: Mean, Median & Mode

Mean, Median & Mode

Example

Find the mean, median, and mode of the data set $\{4, 7, 7, 8, 10, 12\}$.

$$\text{mean} = \frac{4 + 7 + 7 + 8 + 10 + 12}{6} = \frac{48}{6} = 8$$
Sum divided by the count
$$\text{median} = \frac{7 + 8}{2} = 7.5$$
Six values (even count) — average the 3rd and 4th
$$\text{mode} = 7$$
The only value that appears twice

Note the even-count move: with no single middle value, the median is the average of the two middle values—and it doesn't have to be a value in the set.

The Workhorse: sum = mean × count

The mean formula, read backwards, tells you the total. That's the key to every “find the missing value” question:

Example

Amara's mean score on $5$ tests is $84$. Her first four scores are $78$, $82$, $85$, and $89$. What is her fifth score?

$$\text{total needed} = 84 \times 5 = 420$$
Mean $\times$ count gives the required sum
$$78 + 82 + 85 + 89 = 334$$
What the first four contribute
$$420 - 334 = 86$$
The fifth score

Check: $\frac{334 + 86}{5} = \frac{420}{5} = 84$. ✓

The median needs a sorted list. Data in a table or word problem often arrives unsorted—reorder before you count to the middle.

Read the Displays

Dot Plots: Every Point Visible

A dot plot stacks one dot per data point. The plot below shows the number of books $15$ students read over a summer:

Example

What is the median number of books read? With $15$ values, the median is the $\frac{15+1}{2} = 8$th value—count dots from the left:

$$1, \; 3, \; 7, \; 10, \; \ldots$$
Running totals after $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ books
$$\text{median} = 3$$
The 8th, 9th, and 10th dots all sit at $3$ books

Histograms: Counts over Intervals

A histogram groups data into intervals, so individual values are invisible—you can locate the median's interval, but not its exact value. Here, the movies watched last month by $20$ people:

Example

Based on the histogram, which interval contains the median number of movies watched?

  1. A)0–2
  2. B)3–5
  3. C)6–8
  4. D)The median cannot be determined from a histogram
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: The first bar holds only positions $1$ through $5$—the median sits at positions $10$ and $11$.
  • Choice B: With $20$ values, the median averages the $10$th and $11$th. Running totals: $5$, then $13$—both positions land in the $3$–$5$ bar.
  • Choice C: The $6$–$8$ bar starts at position $14$—past the middle.
  • Choice D: Half right: the exact median is unknowable, but its interval is exactly what running totals reveal.

Box Plots: The Five-Number Summary

Each display trades detail for clarity: dot plots show every value, histograms show only interval counts, box plots show only five summary numbers. Ask “what can this display even tell me?” before hunting for an answer.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisOne-Variable Data: Outliers: Mean vs. Median

Outliers: Mean vs. Median

An outlier is a value far from the rest. It affects the two center measures very differently: the mean chases it, because every dollar enters the sum; the median barely notices, because one value shifts the middle position by at most a step.

Example

Five employees at a small firm earn (in thousands): $42, 45, 48, 50, 55$. Then the owner—earning $260$—is added to the data.

Before (5 values)
$$\text{mean} = \frac{240}{5} = 48$$
Sum $240$
$$\text{median} = 48$$
The 3rd of 5 sorted values
After adding 260 (6 values)
$$\text{mean} = \frac{500}{6} \approx 83.3$$
Jumped $35$ — dragged by the outlier
$$\text{median} = \frac{48 + 50}{2} = 49$$
Moved only $1$

One value moved the mean from $48$ to $83.3$—now nobody earns close to the “average.” The median, at $49$, still describes a typical employee. With outliers, the median is the honest center.

Skew: What Mean vs. Median Reveals

Shape of the dataRelationship
Symmetricmean $\approx$ median
Tail of high values (skewed right)mean $>$ median
Tail of low values (skewed left)mean $<$ median
The mean gets pulled toward the tail; the median stays with the crowd.
Example

If the value $260$ is removed from the six-value salary data set above, which of the following will change the most?

  1. A)The mean
  2. B)The median
  3. C)The minimum
  4. D)The mean and median will change equally
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Removing the outlier drops the mean from $\approx 83.3$ back to $48$—a change of about $35$.
  • Choice B: The median slides only from $49$ to $48$—a change of $1$. Middles are stubborn.
  • Choice C: The minimum is $42$ with or without the owner—removing a maximum can't touch it.
  • Choice D: They change by $35$ and $1$—about as unequal as it gets. This choice exists for students who skip the computation.
“Which measure best represents the typical value?” with an outlier present → the median. “Which will change most if the outlier is removed?” → the mean (and the range, if the outlier was an extreme).
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisOne-Variable Data: Spread: Range & Standard Deviation

Spread: Range & Standard Deviation

Range $=$ maximum $-$ minimum: one subtraction, and—like the mean—hostage to outliers. Standard deviation measures the typical distance of values from the mean. The SAT never asks you to compute it; it asks you to compare it by eye:

Smaller standard deviation
Larger standard deviation

Rule: Same scale, more clustered around the mean → smaller standard deviation. More spread out → larger. That comparison is the entire skill.

Example

Class A's quiz scores all fall between $78$ and $82$. Class B's scores range from $60$ to $100$. The mean score in both classes is $80$. Which statement is true?

  1. A)The standard deviation of Class A's scores is smaller than that of Class B's
  2. B)The standard deviation of Class A's scores is larger than that of Class B's
  3. C)The two classes have different mean scores
  4. D)The standard deviations cannot be compared without calculating them
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Class A's scores sit within $2$ points of the mean; Class B's roam up to $20$ away. Tighter cluster, smaller standard deviation.
  • Choice B: Backwards—spread and standard deviation rise together.
  • Choice C: Both means are given as $80$.
  • Choice D: The SAT expects the by-eye comparison—no computation is ever required, or possible, from a summary like this.

Shifting and Scaling the Whole Data Set

TransformationMean & medianRange & standard deviation
Add $k$ to every valueshift by $k$unchanged
Multiply every value by $k$multiply by $k$multiply by $k$
Sliding the data doesn't change how spread out it is; stretching it does.
“The teacher adds $5$ points to every score” moves the mean and median up $5$—and leaves the range and standard deviation exactly where they were. Distances between values don't feel a shift.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisOne-Variable Data: Practice

Practice

Name the game before computing: missing value (sum $=$ mean $\times$ count), display reading (count carefully), outlier contrast (mean vs. median), or spread comparison (cluster vs. scatter).

Practice
  1. 1.

    The mean of four numbers is $15$. Three of the numbers are $11$, $14$, and $18$. What is the fourth number?

    1. A)$17$
    2. B)$15$
    3. C)$14.3$
    4. D)$60$

    The correct answer is A.

    $$15 \times 4 = 60$$
    The four numbers must total $60$
    $$11 + 14 + 18 = 43$$
    The three known numbers
    $$60 - 43 = 17$$
    The fourth number
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Check: $\frac{43 + 17}{4} = 15$. ✓
    • Choice B: $15$ assumes the missing value equals the mean—only true if the other three already averaged $15$ (they average $14.3$).
    • Choice C: $14.3$ is the mean of the three known numbers—a computation about the wrong group.
    • Choice D: $60$ is the required total, one step short of the answer.
  2. 2.

    The dot plot shows the number of pets in $15$ households. What is the median number of pets?

    1. A)$3$
    2. B)$2$
    3. C)$2.8$
    4. D)$4$

    The correct answer is A. With $15$ values the median is the $8$th. Running totals: $2$, then $7$, then $11$—the $8$th dot lands in the stack at $3$.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Positions $8$ through $11$ all hold the value $3$. ✓
    • Choice B: $2$ is the mode—the tallest stack, not the middle dot.
    • Choice C: $2.8$ is the mean ($42 \div 15$)—the right kind of care applied to the wrong measure.
    • Choice D: $4$ is merely the middle of the axis labels $1$–$5$; the data's weight sits lower.
  3. 3.

    A data set consists of the values $12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 18$. If the value $45$ is added to the data set, which of the following measures will increase the most?

    1. A)The mean
    2. B)The median
    3. C)The mode
    4. D)All will increase equally

    The correct answer is A. Before: mean $= \frac{90}{6} = 15$, median $= 15$, mode $= 15$. After adding $45$: mean $= \frac{135}{7} \approx 19.3$, median $=$ the $4$th of $7$ values $= 15$, mode $= 15$.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: The mean climbs about $4.3$—the outlier's entire excess gets averaged in.
    • Choice B: The median doesn't move at all: the middle of the sorted list is still $15$.
    • Choice C: $15$ still appears twice and $45$ once—the mode is untouched.
    • Choice D: One measure moved $4.3$, the other two moved $0$—outliers are precisely the situation where measures disagree.
  4. 4.

    A teacher adds $5$ points to every student's score on a quiz. Which of the following describes the effect on the mean and the standard deviation of the scores?

    1. A)The mean increases by 5; the standard deviation is unchanged
    2. B)The mean and the standard deviation both increase by 5
    3. C)The mean is unchanged; the standard deviation increases by 5
    4. D)The mean and the standard deviation are both unchanged

    The correct answer is A. Every value slides up $5$, so the balance point slides up $5$ with them. But the distances between values—and from each value to the new mean—are identical, so the spread doesn't change.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Shift the data → shift the center, keep the spread. (Multiplying is the operation that would scale both.)
    • Choice B: Standard deviation measures spacing, and adding a constant changes no spacing.
    • Choice C: Exactly reversed: the center moves, the spread stays.
    • Choice D: The mean must rise—every single value did.

Things to Remember

  • Mean $= \frac{\text{sum}}{\text{count}}$, and backwards: sum $=$ mean $\times$ count—the key to missing-value questions.
  • Median: sort first; odd count → middle value, even count → average of the two middles (it may not be in the set).
  • Outliers drag the mean, barely move the median. With an outlier present, the median is the better “typical” value; the mean (and range) change most when it's removed.
  • Mean pulled above the median → tail of high values (skewed right); below → tail of low values.
  • Standard deviation is compared, never computed: tighter cluster around the mean → smaller SD.
  • Add $k$ to every value → center shifts by $k$, spread (range, SD) unchanged. Multiply by $k$ → both scale.
  • Displays: dot plots show every value; histograms show interval counts only (median interval via running totals); box plots show the five-number summary, median = the line in the box.
