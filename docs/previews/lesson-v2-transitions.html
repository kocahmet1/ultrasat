Transitions

Transitions questions give you a short text with a blank—almost always at the start of a sentence—and four transition words or phrases. The stem never changes: Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition? Your real task sits underneath the blank: figure out the logical relationship between the idea before it and the idea after it. The transition is just that relationship's name tag.

These are among the most frequently tested skills in Reading and Writing, and among the most improvable: there are only about nine relationships to know, and the same words signal them every time.

Strategies

  • Cover the choices. Read the sentence before the blank and the entire sentence containing it—choices first is how traps win.
  • Paraphrase the two ideas plainly, then name the arrow between them: does idea two oppose, result from, add to, exemplify, or intensify idea one?
  • Translate the relationship into a category, then find the choice in that category.
  • Plug the winner back in and reread both sentences—the pair should now read as one continuous thought.
  • Eliminate same-category duplicates. If two choices would do the identical job, neither can be the single credited answer.
Transitions connect ideas, not just neighboring sentences. If the local pair feels ambiguous, read one sentence further—the blank sometimes serves the paragraph's whole arc (a conclusion, a final contrast) rather than only the sentence before it.
Expression of IdeasTransitions: The Transition Categories

The Transition Categories

Nearly every transition the SAT tests falls into one of these categories. Learn the categories—not just the words—because the question is really asking which category does this blank need?

CategoryCommon transitionsThe second idea…
Contrasthowever, but, nevertheless, in contrast, on the other hand, converselypushes against the first
Cause & resulttherefore, thus, consequently, as a result, accordinglyfollows from the first
Additionmoreover, furthermore, in addition, alsocontinues in the same direction with something new
Examplefor example, for instance, specificallyis a particular instance of the first
Emphasis / correctionin fact, indeed, that is, in other wordsintensifies, sharpens, or restates the first
Sequence & timethen, next, finally, meanwhile, subsequently, previouslyhappens before, after, or alongside the first
Similaritysimilarly, likewisemakes a parallel point about something comparable
Alternativeinstead, rather, alternativelyreplaces a first idea that was rejected
Concessiongranted, admittedly, of coursegrants a point—usually before pushing back

Watch a single sentence pair accept one category and reject another:

Rain had fallen without pause for three days. the trail was deep in mud.

  • As a result, the trail was deep in mud.
    Mud is the consequence of three days of rain—cause and result.
  • Nevertheless, the trail was deep in mud.
    Nevertheless promises a surprise that defies the rain—a muddy trail is the opposite of surprising here.

Rule: If two answer choices belong to the same category and carry the same nuance—“therefore” and “consequently,” say—eliminate both. Interchangeable choices can't contain the single credited answer.

Expression of IdeasTransitions: Find the Relationship

Find the Relationship

The method, in full. It runs on paraphrase, because transitions link ideas—and you can't name a relationship between two sentences you haven't compressed into ideas yet.

  1. Cover the choices and read up to the end of the sentence containing the blank.
  2. Compress each side into a plain clause. Before: “their blood clots around toxins.” After: “industry uses it to test for contamination.”
  3. Name the arrow. Opposes? Results? Adds? Exemplifies? Intensifies? Say it in your head before looking down.
  4. Match category → choice, then reread the pair with the winner installed.
Example

The blood of the horseshoe crab contains amebocytes, cells that clot almost instantly around even trace amounts of bacterial toxin. the pharmaceutical industry has relied for decades on horseshoe crab blood to screen vaccines and medical devices for contamination.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A)However,
  2. B)Consequently,
  3. C)For instance,
  4. D)Meanwhile,

Compress: the blood detects toxins → industry uses it to detect toxins. The second idea is a direct consequence of the first—the property explains the practice.

Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: However demands opposition, but the second sentence cooperates with the first—no push against anything. Wrong category: contrast.
  • Choice B: Cause and result: because the cells clot around toxin, the industry uses the blood as a toxin detector. Reread with Consequently—the pair snaps into one thought.
  • Choice C: The industry's practice isn't an instance of the blood clotting—it's a downstream effect of it. Wrong category: example.
  • Choice D: Meanwhile stamps the pair as simultaneous events in parallel—but these aren't two events, they're a property and its consequence. Wrong category: time.
Notice how the elimination worked: every wrong choice failed at the category level. You almost never need to weigh two transitions within a category—the SAT separates its four choices across categories precisely so that naming the relationship decides the question.
Expression of IdeasTransitions: Tricky Pairs

Tricky Pairs

A handful of look-alike pairs cause most Transitions mistakes. Each pair splits on one precise question—ask it, and the fog clears:

The pairChoose the first when…Choose the second when…
however / insteadidea two simply opposes idea oneidea one was rejected or negated, and idea two replaces it (“did not X. Instead, Y”)
therefore / for exampleidea two is a conclusion drawn from idea oneidea two is a specific case of idea one's general claim
in fact / for exampleidea two raises the stakes of the same claim (often correcting how things merely seem)idea two drops down from the general claim to one instance
meanwhile / subsequentlytwo things happen at the same time, usually in different placesthe second thing happens after the first
nevertheless / thereforeidea two holds despite idea oneidea two holds because of idea one
Example

To a surface dweller, an animal that glows might seem like a rare curiosity of the deep sea. bioluminescence is closer to the rule than the exception: by one estimate, three-quarters of the animals drifting in the ocean's open midwaters can produce their own light.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A)In fact,
  2. B)For example,
  3. C)Meanwhile,
  4. D)Similarly,

Compress: glowing animals seem rare → they are actually the majority. The second idea doesn't oppose a fact—it corrects an appearance, and raises the stakes with a striking statistic. That is the signature of emphasis / correction.

Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: In fact is built for exactly this move: “things seem X; in fact, the reality is stronger.” The estimate intensifies and corrects the first sentence's impression.
  • Choice B: The 75-percent estimate is not an example of bioluminescence seeming rare—it's evidence against that impression. Wrong category, and the tricky-pair question (“stakes raised, or instance given?”) settles it.
  • Choice C: No simultaneous events anywhere in sight. Wrong category: time.
  • Choice D: Similarly needs a parallel claim about a comparable subject; here both sentences are about the same subject, and the second overturns the first rather than paralleling it. Wrong category.
Never pick a transition because it appears often on the test or sounds sophisticated. However is the most common transition in academic prose—and on any given question it is still wrong three times out of four.
Expression of IdeasTransitions: Practice

Practice

Cover the choices, compress the two ideas, name the arrow, then match. Commit before opening the answer.

Practice
  1. 1.

    Early critics dismissed Emily Dickinson's unconventional punctuation as carelessness, and her first editors quietly “corrected” it, replacing her dashes with standard commas and periods. many scholars regard the dashes as deliberate instruments of rhythm and emphasis, and modern editions restore them.

    Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

    1. A)Similarly,
    2. B)Today, however,
    3. C)For example,
    4. D)As a result,

    The correct answer is B. Compress: early readers treated the dashes as errors → current scholars treat them as artistry. Two eras, opposite judgments: a contrast (with a time shift that Today makes explicit).

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Similarly would extend the dismissal into the present—the text does the opposite. Wrong direction.
    • Choice B: However names the reversal in judgment; Today marks the era change that carries it. Reread the pair—it now reads as one arc from dismissal to restoration.
    • Choice C: The scholars' view is not an instance of the editors' corrections—it's the repudiation of them. Wrong category.
    • Choice D: As a result would make the modern reverence a consequence of the early dismissal—backwards causality the text never suggests. Wrong category.
  2. 2.

    Play is not a uniquely human habit: many animals engage in behavior that has no obvious survival purpose and seems to exist for its own sake. young ravens have been observed sliding down snowbanks on their backs, climbing up, and sliding down again.

    Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

    1. A)In contrast,
    2. B)Therefore,
    3. C)For instance,
    4. D)Nevertheless,

    The correct answer is C. Compress: many animals play → ravens sliding down snowbanks. The second idea is one specific, vivid case of the first sentence's general claim—the example category, on the nose.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: The ravens illustrate the claim; nothing opposes anything. Wrong category: contrast.
    • Choice B: The sliding isn't a conclusion drawn from the claim—it's a case of it. Ask the tricky-pair question: instance, not inference. Wrong category.
    • Choice C: General claim, then a particular animal doing a particular playful thing: For instance is the exact hinge this pair needs.
    • Choice D: Nevertheless concedes and pushes back; the raven anecdote pushes nothing back. Wrong category.
  3. 3.

    Recycling aluminum saves about 95 percent of the energy required to produce the same amount of metal from ore. aluminum can be melted down and reprocessed again and again without any loss of quality, so the savings repeat with every cycle.

    Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

    1. A)Moreover,
    2. B)Instead,
    3. C)For example,
    4. D)Otherwise,

    The correct answer is A. Compress: recycling aluminum saves enormous energy → and it can be recycled endlessly, multiplying the savings. The second idea doesn't oppose, exemplify, or result from the first—it stacks a second advantage on top of it: addition.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Moreover adds a new point in the same direction—exactly the relationship between the one-time saving and the repeatable one.
    • Choice B: Instead needs a rejected first idea to replace; the first sentence stands unchallenged. Wrong category: alternative.
    • Choice C: Close—but reprocessability is a different benefit, not a specific instance of the 95-percent energy claim. Additions continue; examples descend. Wrong category.
    • Choice D: Otherwise warns what happens if the first idea fails to hold—no such warning follows. Wrong category.

Things to Remember

  • The blank is a relationship wearing a word. Name the relationship first; the word follows.
  • Cover the choices, compress the idea before and the idea after into plain clauses, and say the arrow out loud: opposes, results, adds, exemplifies, intensifies.
  • Know the categories cold: contrast, cause & result, addition, example, emphasis, time, similarity, alternative, concession.
  • Two choices doing the identical job eliminate each other.
  • Master the tricky pairs: however vs. instead, therefore vs. for example, in fact vs. for example.
  • Always plug the winner back in and reread the pair as one continuous thought.
