Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is, on a scale from $0$ (impossible) to $1$ (certain). When outcomes are equally likely, it's a counting exercise:

$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\textcolor{#157f4b}{\text{favorable outcomes}}}{\textcolor{#0f6cbd}{\text{total possible outcomes}}}$$
ProbabilityMeaning
$0$impossible
$0.5$as likely as not
$1$certain
$\frac{7}{20}$, $0.35$, $35\%$the same probability in three costumes—convert freely

Strategies

  • The denominator is the group being drawn from. Identifying it correctly is the skill—most wrong answers are right numerators over wrong denominators.
  • Two-way tables: “and” questions take one cell over the grand total.
  • “Given that” shrinks the world: the denominator becomes that row's or column's total, not the grand total.
  • Complement: $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$—often the faster count.
  • Probability predicts counts: expected number $=$ probability $\times$ group size.
Before computing anything, say the denominator out loud: “out of all $200$ students” or “out of the $120$ eleventh-graders.” The words of the question always name the group.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisProbability: Probability Basics

Probability Basics

Example

A jar holds $5$ red, $7$ blue, and $8$ green marbles. One marble is drawn at random. What is the probability it is blue?

$$\text{total} = 5 + 7 + 8 = 20$$
Count all possible outcomes first
$$P(\text{blue}) = \frac{7}{20} = 0.35$$
Favorable over total

The Complement: “Not” Questions

For the same jar, what is $P(\text{not green})$? Two roads, one answer:

Count the favorables
$$P = \frac{5 + 7}{20} = \frac{12}{20}$$
Red and blue are the non-greens
Subtract from 1
$$P = 1 - \frac{8}{20} = \frac{12}{20}$$
Everything except green

Both give $\frac{12}{20} = \frac{3}{5}$. The complement route shines when “not” covers many categories—one subtraction instead of a long addition.

Example

A box contains $12$ pens: $5$ black, $4$ blue, and $3$ red. If one pen is selected at random, what is the probability that it is not blue?

  1. A)$\frac{2}{3}$
  2. B)$\frac{1}{3}$
  3. C)$\frac{5}{12}$
  4. D)$\frac{7}{12}$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Not blue means black or red: $\frac{5 + 3}{12} = \frac{8}{12} = \frac{2}{3}$. Or: $1 - \frac{4}{12}$.
  • Choice B: $\frac{4}{12} = \frac{1}{3}$ is $P(\text{blue})$—the “not” never got applied.
  • Choice C: $\frac{5}{12}$ counts only the black pens, as if “not blue” had one alternative. The red pens are also not blue.
  • Choice D: $\frac{7}{12}$ adds blue and red—a complement taken of the wrong color.
Sanity bound: every probability lives in $[0, 1]$, and $P(A) + P(\text{not } A) = 1$. If your two pieces don't sum to $1$, a count is off.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisProbability: Two-Way Tables

Two-Way Tables

Most SAT probability questions read from a two-way table—counts sorted by two categories at once. A survey of $200$ students, by grade and by whether they play a school sport:

Plays a sportNo sportTotal
Grade 11$72$$48$$120$
Grade 12$18$$62$$80$
Total$90$$110$$200$
Row and column totals in the margins; the grand total ($200$) in the corner.
Example

If one of the $200$ students is chosen at random, what is the probability that the student plays a sport?

$$P(\text{sport}) = \frac{90}{200} = \frac{9}{20}$$
Column total over the grand total — grade doesn't matter here

Rule: An “and” event names one cell: $P(A \text{ and } B) = \dfrac{\text{that cell}}{\text{grand total}}$ — as long as the draw is from the whole group.

Example

If one of the $200$ students is selected at random, what is the probability that the student is in grade 11 and plays a sport?

  1. A)$\frac{9}{25}$
  2. B)$\frac{3}{5}$
  3. C)$\frac{4}{5}$
  4. D)$\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{9}{20}$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: One cell: $72$ grade-11 athletes, out of all $200$ students: $\frac{72}{200} = \frac{9}{25}$.
  • Choice B: $\frac{72}{120} = \frac{3}{5}$ divides by the grade-11 total—that answers a given that question (“given grade 11…”), which this is not.
  • Choice C: $\frac{72}{90} = \frac{4}{5}$ divides by the athletes' total—the other conditional, also not asked.
  • Choice D: Multiplying the two overall probabilities assumes grade and sport are unrelated—the table shows they aren't (grade 11 plays at a much higher rate). Read the cell; don't reconstruct it.
Check a table's health before using it: rows sum to their margins, margins sum to the grand total. Ten seconds, and it also forces you to see where everything lives.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisProbability: “Given That” Shrinks the World

“Given That” Shrinks the World

Rule: Conditional probability: “given that $B$” restricts the draw to group $B$. The denominator becomes $B$'s total; the numerator is the part of $B$ that is also $A$.

Same table as before. Given that a student is in grade 11, what is the probability the student plays a sport? The words “given grade 11” collapse the world to that one row of $120$ students:

$$P(\text{sport} \mid \text{grade 11}) = \frac{72}{120} = \frac{3}{5}$$
Athletes within the row, over the row total

Compare: overall, $P(\text{sport}) = \frac{90}{200} = 45\%$—but within grade 11 it's $60\%$. Conditioning changed the answer because the two grades behave differently. And the direction of conditioning matters too:

$P(\text{sport} \mid \text{grade 11})$
$$\frac{72}{120} = \frac{3}{5}$$
World = the $120$ in grade 11
$P(\text{grade 11} \mid \text{sport})$
$$\frac{72}{90} = \frac{4}{5}$$
World = the $90$ athletes

Same cell on top ($72$), different worlds below—$\frac{3}{5}$ versus $\frac{4}{5}$. The condition always names the denominator.

Example

One student is selected at random from those who do not play a sport. What is the probability that the student is in grade 12?

  1. A)$\frac{31}{55}$
  2. B)$\frac{31}{100}$
  3. C)$\frac{31}{40}$
  4. D)$\frac{11}{20}$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: “From those who do not play” makes the world the $110$ non-athletes; $62$ of them are twelfth-graders: $\frac{62}{110} = \frac{31}{55}$.
  • Choice B: $\frac{62}{200} = \frac{31}{100}$ uses the grand total—but the draw was never from all $200$ students.
  • Choice C: $\frac{62}{80} = \frac{31}{40}$ conditions in the wrong direction: that's $P(\text{no sport} \mid \text{grade 12})$.
  • Choice D: $\frac{110}{200} = \frac{11}{20}$ is just the probability of being a non-athlete—the condition, not the question.
Watch for conditioning in disguise: “of the students who…,” “among those that…,” “selected from…”—all mean given that, and all shrink the denominator.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisProbability: Frequencies & Expected Counts

Frequencies & Expected Counts

Probability from a Frequency Table

One-variable frequency data works the same way—the denominator is the total frequency. Twenty students reported the number of books they read last month:

Books read$0$$1$$2$$3$
Number of students$4$$9$$5$$2$
Example

If one of the $20$ students is chosen at random, what is the probability that the student read at least $2$ books?

  1. A)$\frac{7}{20}$
  2. B)$\frac{1}{4}$
  3. C)$\frac{13}{20}$
  4. D)$\frac{7}{13}$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: “At least $2$” means $2$ or $3$: $\frac{5 + 2}{20} = \frac{7}{20}$.
  • Choice B: $\frac{5}{20} = \frac{1}{4}$ counts only exactly $2$—“at least” includes everything above it.
  • Choice C: $\frac{13}{20}$ is the complement—the students who read fewer than $2$.
  • Choice D: $\frac{7}{13}$ divides favorable by unfavorable. Probabilities compare with the total, never part-to-part.

From Probability to Predicted Counts

Probability runs forward, too: applied to a group, it predicts a count.

$$\text{expected count} = P(\text{event}) \times \text{group size}$$
Example

Quality testing shows that the probability a randomly selected part is defective is $0.02$. About how many defective parts should be expected in a shipment of $1{,}500$?

$$0.02 \times 1{,}500 = 30$$
Probability $\times$ group size — about $30$ defective parts
Words like “expected,” “predicted,” or “about how many” signal this move—and the answer is an estimate, so it needn't divide evenly in real life.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisProbability: Practice

Practice

For every question, name the denominator's group before computing—then check whether a “given that” (in any disguise) shrinks it.

Practice
  1. 1.

    A bag contains $6$ apple, $9$ grape, and $5$ cherry candies. If one candy is drawn at random, what is the probability that it is not grape?

    1. A)$\frac{11}{20}$
    2. B)$\frac{9}{20}$
    3. C)$\frac{3}{10}$
    4. D)$\frac{11}{9}$

    The correct answer is A. Total $= 6 + 9 + 5 = 20$; not-grape $= 6 + 5 = 11$, so $P = \frac{11}{20}$ (equivalently $1 - \frac{9}{20}$).

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Check: $\frac{9}{20} + \frac{11}{20} = 1$. ✓
    • Choice B: $\frac{9}{20}$ is $P(\text{grape})$—the complement was never taken.
    • Choice C: $\frac{6}{20} = \frac{3}{10}$ counts only apple, forgetting cherry is also not grape.
    • Choice D: $\frac{11}{9}$ is greater than $1$—impossible for a probability, and a part-to-part ratio besides.
  2. 2.
    Morning shiftEvening shiftTotal
    Drives$54$$36$$90$
    Public transit$24$$36$$60$
    Total$78$$72$$150$

    The table classifies $150$ workers by commute type and shift. If one worker is selected at random, what is the probability that the worker takes public transit and works the evening shift?

    1. A)$\frac{6}{25}$
    2. B)$\frac{3}{5}$
    3. C)$\frac{1}{2}$
    4. D)$\frac{2}{5}$

    The correct answer is A. “And” names one cell—$36$ transit-evening workers—over the grand total: $\frac{36}{150} = \frac{6}{25}$.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: One cell over $150$. ✓
    • Choice B: $\frac{36}{60} = \frac{3}{5}$ divides by the transit row—that's $P(\text{evening} \mid \text{transit})$, a conditional that wasn't asked.
    • Choice C: $\frac{36}{72} = \frac{1}{2}$ divides by the evening column—the other unasked conditional.
    • Choice D: $\frac{60}{150} = \frac{2}{5}$ is just $P(\text{transit})$—the “and” half is missing.
  3. 3.

    Using the same table: if the selected worker is known to work the morning shift, what is the probability that the worker drives?

    1. A)$\frac{9}{13}$
    2. B)$\frac{9}{25}$
    3. C)$\frac{3}{5}$
    4. D)$\frac{13}{25}$

    The correct answer is A. “Known to work the morning shift” shrinks the world to that column's $78$ workers, of whom $54$ drive: $\frac{54}{78} = \frac{9}{13}$.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Cell over column total—the condition names the denominator. ✓
    • Choice B: $\frac{54}{150} = \frac{9}{25}$ uses the grand total, ignoring the condition entirely.
    • Choice C: $\frac{54}{90} = \frac{3}{5}$ conditions the wrong way: that's $P(\text{morning} \mid \text{drives})$.
    • Choice D: $\frac{78}{150} = \frac{13}{25}$ is the probability of the condition (morning shift), not of driving within it.
  4. 4.

    The probability that a certain team wins any given game is $\frac{3}{8}$. If the team plays $240$ games, about how many games should the team be expected to win?

    1. A)$90$
    2. B)$80$
    3. C)$150$
    4. D)$30$

    The correct answer is A. Expected count $=$ probability $\times$ group size: $\frac{3}{8} \times 240 = 90$.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $240 \div 8 = 30$ per eighth, times $3$. ✓
    • Choice B: $80$ is $\frac{1}{3}$ of $240$—the fraction $\frac{3}{8}$ flipped into “one in three.”
    • Choice C: $150$ is $\frac{5}{8} \times 240$—the expected losses, the complement.
    • Choice D: $30$ is $240 \div 8$: one eighth, with the $3$ never applied.

Things to Remember

  • $P = \frac{\text{favorable}}{\text{total}}$, always between $0$ and $1$. Fractions, decimals, and percents are interchangeable.
  • The denominator is the group being drawn from—say it before you compute.
  • Two-way tables: “$A$ and $B$” $=$ one cell over the grand total. Check that rows and columns sum to their margins.
  • “Given that,” “of those who,” “selected from” → conditional: the denominator becomes that row or column total. Direction matters: $P(A \mid B) \neq P(B \mid A)$.
  • $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$; the two must sum to $1$. “At least $k$” includes $k$ and everything above.
  • Expected count $=$ probability $\times$ group size.
