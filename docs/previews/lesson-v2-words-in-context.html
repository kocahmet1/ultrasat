Words in Context

Words in Context questions test vocabulary the way real reading does: a word means what the text around it forces it to mean. They come in two flavors—one asks you to fill a blank with the best word; the other asks what a word already in the text means there, in that sentence, regardless of what it usually means.

Language in the questionYour task
“Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?”fill the blank using the text's clues
“As used in the text, what does the word ‘X' most nearly mean?”identify the sense this text uses

Both flavors run on the same engine: the text always contains a clue that locks in the answer. You are never being asked to know a word in a vacuum—you're being asked to read the sentence that defines it.

Strategies

  • Cover the choices and read the whole text first—choices are written to sound plausible before you've found the clue.
  • Predict your own word—or at least the charge: positive, negative, or neutral.
  • Find the clue that forces your prediction: a restatement, a contrast, a parallel, a cause.
  • Match, then plug in and reread. The right word is logical and precise—several choices usually fit loosely; one fits exactly.
  • For “most nearly mean” questions, substitute each choice into the sentence—and distrust the word's most familiar meaning.
  • Unknown words in the choices? Eliminate the ones you know don't fit; never pick a word because it sounds sophisticated.
Your prediction doesn't need to be elegant. “Messy,” “still argued about,” “good for plants” are perfect predictions—you're building a target, not writing the answer.
Craft and StructureWords in Context: Predict, Then Match

Predict, Then Match

The core discipline: decide what the blank must mean before the choices can start whispering. Two levels of prediction work:

  1. A word of your own. Read up to the blank's sentence, then finish the thought in plain English.
  2. At minimum, the charge. Is the blank positive, negative, or neutral? Charge alone often eliminates two or three choices before you weigh a single definition.

Then hold every choice against the prediction—not against the vague question “could this word appear in this sentence?” Almost any word could. The test is whether it says what the clue requires.

Example

Despite decades of close study, the function of the narwhal's spiral tusk remains : researchers have proposed that it senses changes in the water, attracts mates, and even stuns prey, but no single explanation has won general acceptance.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

  1. A)predictable
  2. B)contested
  3. C)inconsequential
  4. D)notorious

The colon is a flashing arrow: everything after it restates the blank. Competing proposals, no accepted winner—prediction: unsettled, still argued about.

Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Predictable points the wrong way entirely—a question with three live rival answers is the opposite of predictable. Fails the clue.
  • Choice B: Contested is the prediction in one word: multiple explanations in play, none accepted. The restatement after the colon pays for every letter of it.
  • Choice C: Inconsequential means unimportant—but decades of study and rival theories show scientists care a great deal. Wrong charge and wrong idea.
  • Choice D: Notorious means famous for something bad. The tusk's function is unknown, not disgraceful. A word that merely sounds dramatic. Imprecise.
“Logical and precise” is a two-part standard. On hard questions, two choices will be logical—only one will be precise, matching the clue's exact relationship rather than its general neighborhood.
Craft and StructureWords in Context: Find the Clue

Find the Clue

SAT texts plant their clues in a handful of standard forms. Learn to spot the form, and the blank starts filling itself:

Clue typeSignalsMini example
Restatementcolon, that is, in other words, an appositiveThe finding was an anomaly: nothing like it had ever been recorded.
Contrastbut, however, although, unlike, despite, once … nowAlthough rehearsals were chaotic, the premiere was remarkably polished.
Similarityand, like, just as, equallyLike the estate's meticulously kept gardens, its library was immaculate.
Cause & effectbecause, so, thereforeBecause the trail was unmarked, hikers needed a guide.
Examplefor example, such as, a listThe species is adaptable: it thrives in deserts, marshes, and city parks.
Reversal words flip the charge mid-sentence: hardly, far from, once … now, despite, no longer. Misread one and you'll confidently choose the perfect opposite of the answer.
Example

Although the expedition journal's title page promised an account of the voyage—every landfall, every trade, every storm—the pages inside recorded little more than daily temperature readings.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

  1. A)tedious
  2. B)plausible
  3. C)anonymous
  4. D)exhaustive

Although sets a contrast between the promise and the reality (nearly empty pages). The dash-list—every landfall, every trade, every storm—restates the promise. Prediction: complete, covering everything.

Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Tedious describes the disappointing reality, not the title page's promise—the reversal trap in its natural habitat. Wrong side of the contrast.
  • Choice B: Plausible means believable—the clue list is about coverage, not credibility. Logical-ish, not precise.
  • Choice C: Anonymous answers a question nobody asked; authorship never comes up. No clue supports it.
  • Choice D: Exhaustive—covering everything—is precisely what “every landfall, every trade, every storm” promises, and precisely what the thin reality betrays.
Craft and StructureWords in Context: “Most Nearly Mean” Questions

“Most Nearly Mean” Questions

The second flavor quotes a word already in the text—almost always a common word with several senses: assume, charge, economy, check, bear. The engineered trap is the word's most familiar meaning, sitting in the choices, wrong for this sentence.

Rule: Answer with the sense the text uses, never the sense you know best. Substitute each choice for the word; exactly one preserves the sentence's meaning.

Example

When the conductor fell ill on the eve of the tour, the orchestra's young assistant assumed direction of the ensemble, leading all fourteen performances to warm reviews.

As used in the text, what does the word “assumed” most nearly mean?

  1. A)supposed
  2. B)took on
  3. C)pretended
  4. D)doubted

Substitute each choice into the sentence and listen:

Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: “The assistant supposed direction of the ensemble”—the familiar meaning of assume, and it produces nonsense here. The familiar-meaning trap, working as designed.
  • Choice B: “The assistant took on direction of the ensemble, leading all fourteen performances”—the sentence's logic (stepping in, then leading) survives intact.
  • Choice C: Pretended implies the assistant only faked leadership—yet the performances really happened under his baton. Contradicts the sentence.
  • Choice D: Doubted direction? No sense of assume means doubt, and the warm reviews argue confidence besides. No fit at all.
The substitution reread is not optional. Your memory of what a word “means” is exactly what these questions are built to exploit—the sentence with the choice installed is the only evidence that counts.
Craft and StructureWords in Context: Practice

Practice

Cover the choices, predict (a word or at least a charge), find the clue, match—then plug in and reread. Commit before opening the answer.

Practice
  1. 1.

    The mantis shrimp's strike is astonishingly : its hammer-like club accelerates about as fast as a small-caliber bullet and reaches its target in under three milliseconds.

    Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

    1. A)conspicuous
    2. B)deliberate
    3. C)rapid
    4. D)durable

    The correct answer is C. The colon restates the blank: bullet-like acceleration, a strike in under three milliseconds. Prediction: fast.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Conspicuous means easy to notice—if anything, a three-millisecond strike is nearly invisible. Wrong idea.
    • Choice B: Deliberate means slow and considered—the exact opposite of the clue. Wrong direction.
    • Choice C: Rapid is the restatement's one-word summary: the entire clause after the colon is a description of speed.
    • Choice D: Durable describes toughness over time; the clue measures milliseconds, not wear. Wrong idea.
  2. 2.

    When it opened in 1889, prominent artists condemned the Eiffel Tower as ; today the same silhouette is celebrated as a triumph of iron elegance.

    Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

    1. A)graceful
    2. B)ungainly
    3. C)durable
    4. D)inevitable

    The correct answer is B. Two clues stack: condemned fixes a negative charge, and the then/today reversal demands the opposite of “elegance.” Prediction: ugly, awkward.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Graceful is praise—inside a verb of condemnation. Wrong charge, and the wrong side of the reversal.
    • Choice B: Ungainly—awkward, inelegant—is negative, aimed at appearance, and the precise mirror of the elegance now celebrated.
    • Choice C: Durable is neutral-to-positive and about endurance, not looks; you can't condemn a tower for lasting. Wrong charge, wrong idea.
    • Choice D: Inevitable has nothing to do with the artists' aesthetic complaint—and condemning something as “unavoidable” makes little sense. No clue supports it.
  3. 3.

    The naturalist's field notes betray her excitement at the discovery: exclamation marks crowd every page describing the reef, and whole paragraphs are underlined twice.

    As used in the text, what does the word “betray” most nearly mean?

    1. A)deceive
    2. B)abandon
    3. C)undermine
    4. D)reveal

    The correct answer is D. Substitute and reread: the exclamation marks and double underlines show her excitement. The notes are making her feelings visible—despite betray's more familiar, treacherous senses.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: “The notes deceive her excitement” doesn't parse—and nothing here is being tricked. The familiar-meaning trap.
    • Choice B: Abandon her excitement? The pages overflow with it. Contradicts the evidence after the colon.
    • Choice C: Undermine means weaken—but the punctuation amplifies her excitement rather than sapping it. Wrong direction.
    • Choice D: “The notes reveal her excitement: exclamation marks crowd every page…” The colon's evidence flows perfectly from it—this is betray in its give-away sense.

Things to Remember

  • Cover the choices and predict first—a plain word of your own, or at least the charge (positive, negative, neutral).
  • The clue is always in the text: restatement, contrast, similarity, cause, or example. Find the form, and the blank fills itself.
  • Reversal words (hardly, far from, once … now, despite) flip the charge—misread one and you'll pick the perfect opposite.
  • The standard is logical and precise: several choices fit loosely; one matches the clue exactly.
  • On “most nearly mean” questions, substitute every choice into the sentence—and distrust the word's most familiar meaning.
  • Never choose a word for its sophistication. The test rewards the reader, not the thesaurus.
