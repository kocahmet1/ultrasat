Ratios, Rates, and Proportions

A ratio compares two quantities ($3$ cups of flour to $2$ of sugar). A rate is a ratio of quantities with different units (miles per gallon). A proportion says two ratios are equal—and it's the engine that solves most of this domain's questions.

The math here is light: multiply, divide, cross-multiply. The points are won and lost on bookkeeping—which quantity is on top, what the whole is, and whether the units make sense. Let the labels do the thinking.

Strategies

  • Write every ratio as a labeled fraction and keep the same order throughout: $\frac{\text{red}}{\text{blue}}$ stays $\frac{\text{red}}{\text{blue}}$.
  • Part : part vs. part : whole. A $3{:}5$ ratio has a whole of $3 + 5 = 8$ parts—check which comparison the question gives and which it asks for.
  • “Per” means divide, and the unit after per goes in the denominator. A unit rate is the amount per one.
  • Proportions: match units across the tops and across the bottoms, then cross-multiply.
  • Conversions: multiply by conversion factors—fractions equal to $1$—arranged so the old unit cancels.
  • End with a units check. If the units of your answer are wrong, the setup was wrong—no exceptions.
Units are self-checking machinery. Carry them through every step and cancel them like factors—when the leftover unit matches what the question asks for, the arithmetic is almost always right too.

Ratios: Parts and Wholes

The ratio $a{:}b$ means “$a$ of this for every $b$ of that,” and it can be written as the fraction $\frac{a}{b}$. One ratio generates a whole family of comparisons—suppose a bag has red and blue marbles in the ratio $3{:}5$:

ComparisonRatioAs a fraction
red to blue (part : part)$3:5$$\frac{3}{5}$
red to all (part : whole)$3:8$$\frac{3}{8}$
blue to all (part : whole)$5:8$$\frac{5}{8}$
The whole is the sum of the parts: $3 + 5 = 8$.

From Ratio to Actual Counts

A ratio gives proportions, not counts—the actual numbers are the ratio scaled up by some factor $x$: $3x$ red and $5x$ blue. The total pins down $x$.

Example

At an animal shelter, the ratio of dogs to cats is $4:7$. If the shelter has $44$ dogs and cats in total, how many cats are there?

$$4x + 7x = 44$$
Dogs $= 4x$, cats $= 7x$ — the parts sum to the total
$$11x = 44 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 4$$
Each “part” is $4$ animals
$$7x = 28 \;\text{cats}$$
Scale the cats' share

Check: $16$ dogs $+\;28$ cats $= 44$, and $\frac{16}{28} = \frac{4}{7}$. ✓

Example

A juice blend mixes cranberry juice and apple juice in a ratio of $2:3$. How many cups of cranberry juice are in $20$ cups of the blend?

  1. A)$8$
  2. B)$12$
  3. C)$2$
  4. D)$10$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: The whole is $2 + 3 = 5$ parts, so each part is $20 \div 5 = 4$ cups; cranberry gets $2 \times 4 = 8$.
  • Choice B: $12$ is the apple juice—the other part. Answer the part that was asked.
  • Choice C: $2$ is the raw ratio number, never scaled up to the actual $20$-cup batch.
  • Choice D: $10$ splits the blend in half—ignoring the $2:3$ ratio entirely.
The single most common ratio error: treating a part : part ratio as part : whole. $2:3$ cranberry-to-apple means cranberry is $\frac{2}{5}$ of the blend—not $\frac{2}{3}$.

Rates and Unit Rates

A rate compares different units—miles and gallons, pages and minutes, dollars and ounces. The most useful version is the unit rate: the amount per one unit, found by dividing.

Example

A car travels $312$ miles on $12$ gallons of gas. At this rate, how far can it travel on $8$ gallons?

$$\frac{312 \;\text{miles}}{12 \;\text{gallons}} = 26 \;\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{gallon}}$$
Unit rate: divide by the gallons to get miles per one gallon
$$26 \times 8 = 208 \;\text{miles}$$
Scale the unit rate by the new amount

Unit Rates Compare Deals

Two prices with different sizes can't be compared directly—but their unit prices can:

18 oz jar — $\$4.50$
$$\frac{\$4.50}{18 \;\text{oz}} = \$0.25 \;\text{per oz}$$
Divide price by amount
24 oz jar — $\$5.52$
$$\frac{\$5.52}{24 \;\text{oz}} = \$0.23 \;\text{per oz}$$
Lower cost per ounce — the better buy
“Per” tells you the division order: dollars per ounce = $\frac{\text{dollars}}{\text{ounces}}$, miles per hour = $\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hours}}$. Whatever follows per lives in the denominator—and speed problems are just this rule: distance $=$ rate $\times$ time.

Proportions & Proportional Relationships

A proportion sets two ratios equal. To build one safely, make the units match across the tops and across the bottoms—then cross-multiply.

Rule: If $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$, then $ad = bc$ (cross-multiplication).

Example

A recipe uses $3$ cups of flour to make $24$ muffins. How many cups are needed for $40$ muffins?

$$\frac{3 \;\text{cups}}{24 \;\text{muffins}} = \frac{f \;\text{cups}}{40 \;\text{muffins}}$$
Cups over muffins on both sides
$$24f = 120$$
Cross-multiply
$$f = 5 \;\text{cups}$$
Divide by $24$

Proportional Relationships: $y = kx$

Two quantities are directly proportional when their ratio never changes: $y = kx$, where $k$ (the constant of proportionality) is just the unit rate. The graph is a line through the origin—double the input, double the output.

Example

The quantity $y$ is directly proportional to $x$, and $y = 15$ when $x = 6$. What is the value of $y$ when $x = 10$?

  1. A)$4$
  2. B)$19$
  3. C)$25$
  4. D)$90$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: $4$ uses the constant upside down: $10 \times \frac{6}{15}$. The ratio is $\frac{y}{x}$, not $\frac{x}{y}$.
  • Choice B: $19$ is additive thinking: $x$ went up by $4$, so $y$ goes up by $4$. Proportional quantities scale by multiplication, not addition.
  • Choice C: $k = \frac{15}{6} = 2.5$, so $y = 2.5(10) = 25$. (Or the proportion $\frac{15}{6} = \frac{y}{10}$.)
  • Choice D: $90$ multiplies $15 \times 6$—two values of different quantities multiplied for no reason.
The additive trap (choice B) is this skill's signature wrong answer. When quantities are proportional, ask “what do I multiply by?”—never “what do I add?”
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisRatios, Rates, and Proportions: Unit Conversions

Unit Conversions

A conversion factor is a fraction equal to $1$—like $\frac{100 \;\text{cm}}{1 \;\text{m}}$—so multiplying by it changes the units without changing the quantity. Arrange each factor so the unwanted unit sits in the denominator and cancels:

Example

How many centimeters is $2$ meters? ($1$ m $= 100$ cm)

$$2 \;\cancel{\text{m}} \times \frac{100 \;\text{cm}}{1 \;\cancel{\text{m}}} = 200 \;\text{cm}$$
Old unit cancels; only cm survives

Chain Factors for Multi-Step Conversions

Example

How many hours is $7{,}200$ seconds?

$$7{,}200 \;\cancel{\text{s}} \times \frac{1 \;\cancel{\text{min}}}{60 \;\cancel{\text{s}}} \times \frac{1 \;\text{h}}{60 \;\cancel{\text{min}}}$$
Seconds → minutes → hours; each factor kills one unit
$$\frac{7{,}200}{3{,}600} = 2 \;\text{h}$$
Multiply the chain through

Converting a Rate: Fix Both Units

A rate has a unit on top and bottom—convert each with its own factor:

Example

A train travels $72$ kilometers per hour. What is its speed in meters per second?

$$\frac{72 \;\cancel{\text{km}}}{1 \;\cancel{\text{h}}} \times \frac{1{,}000 \;\text{m}}{1 \;\cancel{\text{km}}} \times \frac{1 \;\cancel{\text{h}}}{3{,}600 \;\text{s}}$$
One factor converts the top (km → m), one the bottom (h → s)
$$\frac{72{,}000}{3{,}600} = 20 \;\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$$
Only m on top and s below survive

Density and Other Built-In Rates

Density is a rate in disguise—mass per unit of volume: $\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}$.

Example

A metal block has a mass of $540$ grams and a volume of $60$ cubic centimeters. What is its density in grams per cubic centimeter?

$$\text{density} = \frac{540 \;\text{g}}{60 \;\text{cm}^3} = 9 \;\frac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}$$
The unit asked for — grams per cm³ — dictates the division
Whenever a conversion confuses you, write the units first and the numbers second: the unit you want to eliminate goes in a denominator, the unit you want to keep goes on top. The numbers just come along for the ride.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisRatios, Rates, and Proportions: Practice

Practice

For each one: label the ratio, identify part vs. whole, and let the units steer. Commit before opening the answer.

Practice
  1. 1.

    A bag contains only red and green candies in a ratio of $5:3$. If the bag holds $48$ candies, how many are green?

    1. A)$18$
    2. B)$30$
    3. C)$3$
    4. D)$16$

    The correct answer is A. The whole is $5 + 3 = 8$ parts:

    $$8x = 48 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 6$$
    Each part is $6$ candies
    $$3x = 18 \;\text{green}$$
    Green owns $3$ of the $8$ parts
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Check: $30$ red $+ 18$ green $= 48$, and $\frac{30}{18} = \frac{5}{3}$. ✓
    • Choice B: $30$ is the red count—the other part of the ratio.
    • Choice C: $3$ is the unscaled ratio number—a ratio is a recipe, not a count.
    • Choice D: $16$ is $48 \div 3$: dividing by green's ratio number instead of scaling by the whole's $8$ parts.
  2. 2.

    A printer prints $45$ pages in $2.5$ minutes. At this rate, how many pages does it print in $8$ minutes?

    1. A)$90$
    2. B)$144$
    3. C)$360$
    4. D)$14$

    The correct answer is B. Unit rate first:

    $$\frac{45 \;\text{pages}}{2.5 \;\text{min}} = 18 \;\frac{\text{pages}}{\text{min}}$$
    Pages per one minute
    $$18 \times 8 = 144 \;\text{pages}$$
    Scale to $8$ minutes
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $90$ doubles the $45$—that covers only $5$ minutes, not $8$.
    • Choice B: $18$ pages per minute for $8$ minutes. (The proportion $\frac{45}{2.5} = \frac{p}{8}$ gives the same $144$.)
    • Choice C: $360$ is $45 \times 8$—it treats $45$ as a per-minute rate and ignores the $2.5$.
    • Choice D: $14$ comes from the proportion set up upside down ($45 \times \frac{2.5}{8}$)—units would be pages·min², which is the tell.
  3. 3.

    On an architectural drawing, $0.5$ inch represents $4$ feet. A wall is $22$ feet long. What is the length, in inches, of the wall on the drawing?

    1. A)$2.75$
    2. B)$11$
    3. C)$44$
    4. D)$5.5$

    The correct answer is A. Inches over feet on both sides:

    $$\frac{0.5 \;\text{in}}{4 \;\text{ft}} = \frac{L \;\text{in}}{22 \;\text{ft}}$$
    Matching units across tops and bottoms
    $$4L = 11 \;\Rightarrow\; L = 2.75 \;\text{in}$$
    Cross-multiply, then divide
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $22$ ft is $5.5$ four-foot chunks, each worth $0.5$ in: $5.5 \times 0.5 = 2.75$. ✓
    • Choice B: $11$ multiplies $22 \times 0.5$, ignoring that each half-inch covers four feet.
    • Choice C: $44$ divides by $0.5$—the scale flipped upside down, making the drawing bigger than the wall.
    • Choice D: $5.5$ is the number of $4$-ft chunks—a correct middle step, stopped one multiplication early.
  4. 4.

    Water flows through a hose at $4.5$ liters per minute. What is this flow rate in milliliters per second? ($1$ L $= 1{,}000$ mL)

    1. A)$75$
    2. B)$270$
    3. C)$4{,}500$
    4. D)$0.075$

    The correct answer is A. Convert the top and the bottom, each with its own factor:

    $$\frac{4.5 \;\cancel{\text{L}}}{1 \;\cancel{\text{min}}} \times \frac{1{,}000 \;\text{mL}}{1 \;\cancel{\text{L}}} \times \frac{1 \;\cancel{\text{min}}}{60 \;\text{s}}$$
    L → mL on top; min → s below
    $$\frac{4{,}500}{60} = 75 \;\frac{\text{mL}}{\text{s}}$$
    Only mL and s survive
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Both units converted, both directions correct. ✓
    • Choice B: $270$ multiplies by $60$ instead of dividing—seconds are smaller than minutes, so the per-second rate must be smaller.
    • Choice C: $4{,}500$ converts the liters but forgets the time unit entirely—that's mL per minute.
    • Choice D: $0.075$ divides by $1{,}000$ and by $60$: the mL factor was applied upside down.

Things to Remember

  • Label every ratio and keep the order fixed. A part : part ratio of $a{:}b$ lives inside a whole of $a + b$ parts—actual counts are $ax$ and $bx$.
  • “Per” = divide, with the unit after per in the denominator. Unit rate = amount per one; it's also $k$ in $y = kx$.
  • Proportions: units must match across tops and across bottoms—then cross-multiply. Proportional graphs pass through the origin.
  • Scale by multiplying, never by adding the difference—the additive trap is this skill's favorite wrong answer.
  • Conversion factors are fractions equal to $1$: put the unit you're eliminating in the denominator and cancel. Rates need one factor for the top and one for the bottom.
  • Density $= \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}$—and in general, the requested unit ($\frac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}$, $\frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}}$) spells out exactly what to divide.
  • Finish with the units check: right units out = right setup in.
