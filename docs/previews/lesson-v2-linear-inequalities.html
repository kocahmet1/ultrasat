Linear Inequalities

An inequality compares two expressions instead of equating them. Where an equation usually pins down one value, an inequality's solutions form a range (in one variable) or a whole region (in two variables)—so the right mental picture is a shaded number line or a shaded half-plane, not a single point.

SymbolRead asWords that signal it
$<$less than“less than,” “fewer than,” “below”
$\leq$less than or equal to“at most,” “no more than,” “a maximum of”
$>$greater than“more than,” “greater than,” “exceeds”
$\geq$greater than or equal to“at least,” “no fewer than,” “a minimum of”

Strategies

  • Solve like an equation—with one extra rule: multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative flips the inequality sign.
  • Translate words precisely. “At least” and “at most” include equality ($\geq$, $\leq$); “more than” and “fewer than” do not ($>$, $<$).
  • A point satisfies an inequality if substituting gives a true statement; for a system it must satisfy every inequality.
  • On graphs: solid boundary for $\leq$ / $\geq$, dashed for $<$ / $>$; shade the side where a test point works.
  • Sanity-check with an easy number from your solution range—it takes five seconds and catches sign-flip errors.
Inequality answers are sets, so the question always narrows them somehow: “which inequality represents…,” “which point satisfies…,” or “what is the greatest/least value….” Spot which narrowing is being asked before you compute.
AlgebraLinear Inequalities: Solve One-Variable Inequalities

Solve One-Variable Inequalities

Use exactly the moves you know from equations—add, subtract, multiply, divide on both sides. The solution is a range, drawn on a number line with a circle and a ray.

Example

Which values of $x$ satisfy $3x + 5 < 20$?

$$3x + 5 < 20$$
Given inequality
$$3x < 15$$
Subtract $5$ from both sides
$$x < 5$$
Divide both sides by $3$ (positive — no flip)

Check with an easy number from the range, like $x = 0$: $3(0) + 5 = 5 < 20$ ✓

The Flip Rule

Rule: Multiplying or dividing both sides of an inequality by a negative number reverses the inequality sign. (Adding or subtracting never flips it.)

Example

Which values of $x$ satisfy $12 - 2x \geq 4$?

$$12 - 2x \geq 4$$
Given inequality
$$-2x \geq -8$$
Subtract $12$ from both sides
$$x \leq 4$$
Divide by $-2$ — flip the sign
  • $-2x \geq -8 \;\Rightarrow\; x \leq 4$
    Divided by $-2$ and flipped. Check $x = 0$: $12 - 0 \geq 4$ is true, and $0 \leq 4$ agrees.
  • $-2x \geq -8 \;\Rightarrow\; x \geq 4$
    Divided by a negative without flipping. Check $x = 5$: $12 - 10 = 2 \geq 4$ is false — the range is wrong.
You can dodge the flip entirely: from $12 - 2x \geq 4$, add $2x$ to both sides to get $12 \geq 4 + 2x$, then $8 \geq 2x$, so $4 \geq x$. Same answer, no negative division, no flip to forget.
AlgebraLinear Inequalities: Translate Words into Inequalities

Translate Words into Inequalities

Most SAT inequality questions are word problems about limits: budgets, capacities, minimum goals. The equation-building skeleton is the same as before—what's new is choosing the right symbol, and the strict/inclusive distinction does real work.

Example

Amir has $\$60$ to spend at the movies. A ticket costs $\$14$, and snacks cost $\$8$ each. What is the greatest number of snacks he can buy?

“Has $\$60$ to spend” means the total can't exceed $60$—spending at most $\$60$:

$$14 + 8s \leq 60$$
Ticket plus $8$ per snack, capped at $60$
$$8s \leq 46$$
Subtract $14$ from both sides
$$s \leq 5.75$$
Divide by $8$

Snacks come in whole numbers, so the greatest value is $s = \textcolor{#157f4b}{5}$—round down, because $6$ snacks would cost $14 + 48 = \$62$, over budget. A fractional cutoff always rounds toward the allowed side.

Example

A freight elevator can carry at most $2{,}000$ pounds. The operator weighs $180$ pounds, and each crate weighs $65$ pounds. Which inequality gives all possible numbers of crates $c$ the elevator can carry with the operator aboard?

  1. A)$65c + 180 \leq 2{,}000$
  2. B)$65c + 180 < 2{,}000$
  3. C)$65c + 180 \geq 2{,}000$
  4. D)$65(c + 180) \leq 2{,}000$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Total weight is $65$ per crate plus the operator's $180$, and “at most” allows equality: $\leq 2{,}000$. (In fact $c = 28$ hits $2{,}000$ exactly—and it's allowed.)
  • Choice B: The strict $<$ wrongly forbids loading exactly $2{,}000$ pounds—but “at most $2{,}000$” includes $2{,}000$.
  • Choice C: Flipped direction: this demands the elevator be loaded to at least its limit.
  • Choice D: Grouping error: it multiplies the operator's weight by $65$ too. Only crates cost $65$ pounds each.
Strict vs. inclusive is a favorite trap: “at most” and “at least” include the boundary ($\leq$, $\geq$); “fewer than” and “more than” exclude it ($<$, $>$). One word changes the symbol.
AlgebraLinear Inequalities: Two Variables: Shade a Half-Plane

Two Variables: Shade a Half-Plane

A two-variable inequality like $y \leq x + 1$ is satisfied by every point on one side of a line. Its graph has two parts: the boundary line $y = x + 1$, and the shaded half-plane of solutions.

Rule: Boundary: solid for $\leq$ or $\geq$ (points on the line count), dashed for $<$ or $>$ (they don't). Solved for $y$: shade above for $y >$, below for $y <$.

$y \leq x + 1$ — solid, shade below
$y > -x + 2$ — dashed, shade above

The Test-Point Method

Not sure which side to shade—or which side a graph is shading? Test one easy point. The origin $(0, 0)$ is ideal whenever it's not on the boundary: for $y \leq x + 1$, testing gives $0 \leq 1$, true—so the solution side is the side containing the origin.

  • Is $(3, 5)$ a solution of $y < 3x - 2$? Test: $5 < 3(3) - 2 = 7$
    True — the point lies in the shaded region.
  • Is $(2, 5)$ a solution of $y < 3x - 2$? Test: $5 < 3(2) - 2 = 4$
    False — the point lies on the wrong side of the boundary.
On the SAT you rarely need to draw the region—testing the given points against the inequality is almost always faster than graphing.
AlgebraLinear Inequalities: Systems of Inequalities

Systems of Inequalities

A system of inequalities asks for points that satisfy all of its inequalities at once. Graphically, that's where the shaded regions overlap:

Example
$$\begin{cases} y \leq x + 3 \\ y \geq -x + 1 \end{cases}$$

Which ordered pair $(x, y)$ satisfies the system above?

  1. A)$(3, 2)$
  2. B)$(0, 4)$
  3. C)$(-2, -3)$
  4. D)$(-4, 8)$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: First: $2 \leq 3 + 3$ ✓ Second: $2 \geq -3 + 1$ ✓ — true in both.
  • Choice B: Second holds ($4 \geq 1$), but the first fails: $4 \leq 0 + 3$ is false. One out of two is out.
  • Choice C: First holds ($-3 \leq 1$), but the second fails: $-3 \geq 2 + 1$ is false. Same trap, other inequality.
  • Choice D: The second holds ($8 \geq 4 + 1$), but the first fails badly: $8 \leq -4 + 3 = -1$ is false. One failed inequality disqualifies the pair—no matter how comfortably the other one passes.

Two Constraints in Context

Real-world systems pair a goal (“earn at least…”) with a limit (“work at most…”). Mara tutors for $\$25$ per hour and lifeguards for $\$12$ per hour. She wants to earn at least $\$300$ while working at most $20$ hours:

$$\begin{cases} 25t + 12l \geq 300 \\ t + l \leq 20 \end{cases}$$
$t$ = tutoring hours · $l$ = lifeguarding hours
  • $t = 8,\; l = 12$: earnings $25(8) + 12(12) = 344 \geq 300$; hours $8 + 12 = 20 \leq 20$
    Works—and note that exactly $20$ hours is allowed, because $\leq$ includes the boundary.
  • $t = 6,\; l = 10$: earnings $25(6) + 12(10) = 270$
    Under the $\$300$ goal — fails the first inequality, so the pair fails the system.
Check candidates against every inequality, every time. Wrong answers on system questions almost always satisfy exactly one of the two.
AlgebraLinear Inequalities: Practice

Practice

Watch for the two classic traps as you work: a missed sign flip, and a boundary treated as strict when it isn't (or the reverse).

Practice
  1. 1.

    Which of the following represents all solutions of $7 - 4x > 19$?

    1. A)$x < -3$
    2. B)$x > -3$
    3. C)$x < 3$
    4. D)$x > 3$

    The correct answer is A.

    $$-4x > 12$$
    Subtract $7$ from both sides
    $$x < -3$$
    Divide by $-4$ — flip the sign
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Check $x = -4$: $7 - 4(-4) = 23 > 19$ ✓
    • Choice B: Divided by $-4$ without flipping. Check $x = 0$: $7 > 19$ is false, so the range containing $0$ can't be right.
    • Choice C: Loses the negative: treats $-4x > 12$ as $4x > 12$ and then flips anyway—two errors trading places.
    • Choice D: Loses the negative and skips the flip: solves $4x > 12$ directly.
  2. 2.

    Jae has saved $\$140$ and saves an additional $\$35$ each week. He wants more than $\$450$ for a new laptop setup. Which inequality gives the numbers of weeks $w$ after which Jae will have more than $\$450$?

    1. A)$140 + 35w > 450$
    2. B)$140 + 35w \geq 450$
    3. C)$35 + 140w > 450$
    4. D)$35w > 450 + 140$

    The correct answer is A. Total savings = starting $\$140$ plus $\$35$ per week—and “more than” is the strict $>$.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $140 + 35w > 450$. (Sanity check: $w = 9$ gives $140 + 315 = 455 > 450$ ✓, while $w = 8$ gives $140 + 280 = 420$, still short.)
    • Choice B: $\geq$ would allow hitting exactly $\$450$—but “more than $\$450$” excludes $450$ itself.
    • Choice C: Swaps the roles: it saves $\$140$ per week starting from $\$35$.
    • Choice D: Adds the starting $\$140$ to the goal instead of to Jae's savings—the $140$ helps him, it doesn't raise the target.
  3. 3.
    $$\begin{cases} y \geq 2x - 3 \\ y < x + 2 \end{cases}$$

    Which ordered pair $(x, y)$ satisfies the system above?

    1. A)$(1, 1)$
    2. B)$(0, 3)$
    3. C)$(3, 1)$
    4. D)$(2, 4)$

    The correct answer is A. Test each pair in both inequalities.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: First: $1 \geq 2(1) - 3 = -1$ ✓ Second: $1 < 1 + 2 = 3$ ✓
    • Choice B: First holds ($3 \geq -3$), but the second fails: $3 < 0 + 2$ is false.
    • Choice C: Second holds ($1 < 5$), but the first fails: $1 \geq 2(3) - 3 = 3$ is false.
    • Choice D: First holds ($4 \geq 1$)—but $(2, 4)$ lands exactly on the boundary $y = x + 2$, and the strict $<$ excludes the line: $4 < 4$ is false.
  4. 4.

    A robotics club has raised $\$96$ and sells raffle tickets for $\$4$ each. The club's goal is to have at least $\$200$. What is the minimum number of tickets the club must sell to reach its goal?

    1. A)$25$
    2. B)$26$
    3. C)$50$
    4. D)$104$

    The correct answer is B.

    $$96 + 4t \geq 200$$
    Current funds plus $4$ per ticket, goal included
    $$4t \geq 104$$
    Subtract $96$ from both sides
    $$t \geq 26$$
    Divide by $4$ — minimum $26$ tickets
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $25$ tickets give $96 + 100 = 196 < 200$—just short. Rounding down only works for maximum questions.
    • Choice B: $96 + 4(26) = 200$ exactly—and “at least $\$200$” includes $200$, so $26$ reaches the goal.
    • Choice C: $50$ is $200 \div 4$: it ignores the $\$96$ already raised.
    • Choice D: $104$ is the missing dollar amount—the subtraction result, never divided by the ticket price.

Things to Remember

  • Solve inequalities with equation moves, plus the flip rule: reverse the sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative. Adding and subtracting never flip.
  • Translate exactly: “at least” $\to \geq$, “at most” $\to \leq$, “more than” $\to >$, “fewer than” $\to <$. The boundary is included only by $\leq$ and $\geq$.
  • Whole-number contexts: round down for maximum questions, up for minimum ones—always toward the allowed side.
  • Two variables: solid boundary for $\leq/\geq$, dashed for $</>$; shade the side where a test point (try the origin) makes the inequality true.
  • Systems: a pair must satisfy every inequality; traps typically pass exactly one, or sit on a strict boundary.
  • Always end with a five-second check: plug one easy value from your answer back into the original inequality.
