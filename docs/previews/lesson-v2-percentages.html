Percentages

A percent is a fraction with a fixed denominator of $100$: $35\% = \frac{35}{100} = 0.35$. Every percent question on the SAT is built from one sentence with three slots—and the question hides exactly one of them:

Part=Percent (as a decimal)×Whole

Strategies

  • Convert to a decimal before computing: drop the $\%$ and divide by $100$. ($35\% \to 0.35$, $4\% \to 0.04$.)
  • Identify the missing slot—the part, the percent, or the whole—and solve the sentence for it.
  • Translate word by word: “of” means multiply, “is” means equals, “what percent” means $\frac{x}{100}$.
  • Percent change = one multiplier: up $r\%$ → $\times(1 + r)$; down $r\%$ → $\times(1 - r)$. To undo a change, divide by the multiplier.
  • Always ask: percent of WHAT? The base matters—and successive percent changes multiply, never add.
Sanity anchors: $10\%$ is “move the decimal,” $50\%$ is half, $1\%$ is a hundredth. Estimate with these before computing—an answer that's wildly off the anchor is wrong regardless of the algebra.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisPercentages: The Three Percent Questions

The Three Percent Questions

The sentence part $=$ percent $\times$ whole generates exactly three question types, depending on which slot is blank:

Missing slotQuestion sounds likeSetupAnswer
Part“What is $30\%$ of $80$?”$p = 0.30 \times 80$$24$
Percent“$18$ is what percent of $45$?”$\frac{18}{45} \times 100$$40\%$
Whole“$36$ is $45\%$ of what number?”$36 = 0.45 \times w$$80$

The first two are direct arithmetic. The third—finding the whole—is where the traps live, because the given number is the part, and dividing feels backwards:

Example

$36$ is $45\%$ of what number?

$$36 = 0.45\,w$$
Part $=$ percent $\times$ whole — the $36$ is the part
$$w = \frac{36}{0.45} = 80$$
Divide by the percent to recover the whole

Check: $0.45 \times 80 = 36$. ✓ The whole ($80$) is bigger than the part ($36$)—as it must be when the percent is under $100\%$.

Example

In a school club, $24$ students play an instrument. These students make up $15\%$ of the club's membership. How many members does the club have?

  1. A)$160$
  2. B)$3.6$
  3. C)$36$
  4. D)$1{,}600$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: $24 = 0.15m$, so $m = 24 \div 0.15 = 160$. Sanity check: $10\%$ of $160$ is $16$, plus half of that is $24$. ✓
  • Choice B: $3.6$ is $15\%$ of $24$—the sentence run in reverse. The $24$ is the part, not the whole.
  • Choice C: $36$ multiplies $24$ by $1.5$, misreading $15\%$ as “one and a half times.”
  • Choice D: $1{,}600$ divides by $0.015$—a decimal-place slip. A club of $1{,}600$ for $24$ musicians fails the sanity check instantly.
“$A$ is $r\%$ of $B$”: the number next to of is the whole. Getting the part and the whole reversed is this page's entire trap inventory.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisPercentages: Percent Change & Multipliers

Percent Change & Multipliers

Increases and decreases are cleanest as one multiplication. Keeping $100\%$ and adding or removing $r$ percent means multiplying by:

$$\text{increase: } \times\,(1 + r) \qquad\qquad \text{decrease: } \times\,(1 - r)$$
ChangeMultiplier
increase by $25\%$$\times\, 1.25$
decrease by $30\%$$\times\, 0.70$
increase by $100\%$ (doubles)$\times\, 2$
decrease by $15\%$$\times\, 0.85$
Example

An $\$80$ jacket is on sale at $30\%$ off. Two roads to the sale price:

One multiplier
$$80 \times 0.70 = 56$$
$30\%$ off = keep $70\%$
Subtract the discount
$$0.30 \times 80 = 24$$
The discount in dollars
$$80 - 24 = 56$$
Same answer, one extra step

Finding the Percent Change

$$\%\ \text{change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\textcolor{#0f6cbd}{\text{original}}} \times 100$$
The denominator is always the original — the “before” value.
Example

The price of a concert ticket rose from $\$60$ to $\$75$. By what percent did the price increase?

  1. A)$25\%$
  2. B)$20\%$
  3. C)$15\%$
  4. D)$125\%$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Change is $\$15$ on an original of $\$60$: $\frac{15}{60} = 0.25 = 25\%$.
  • Choice B: $\frac{15}{75} = 20\%$ divides by the new price—the before/after mix-up this formula exists to prevent.
  • Choice C: $15$ is the change in dollars; a percent needs it compared with the original.
  • Choice D: $\frac{75}{60} = 125\%$ is the new price as a percent of the old one—the price is $125\%$ of what it was, which is a $25\%$ increase.
“Percent of” vs. “percent more”: $75$ is $125\%$ of $60$, and $25\%$ more than $60$. Both sentences describe the same numbers—the question's wording picks which one it wants.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisPercentages: Working Backward: Before the Change

Working Backward: Before the Change

When you know the after and need the before, run the multiplier in reverse: divide by it. The tempting alternative—adding the percent back—uses the wrong base and always misses.

Example

After a $20\%$ discount, a sweater costs $\$44$. What was the original price?

$$0.80\,p = 44$$
Sale price = $80\%$ of the original
$$p = \frac{44}{0.80} = 55$$
Divide by the multiplier — the original was $\$55$
  • $44 \div 0.80 = 55$, and the check runs forward: $55 \times 0.80 = 44$
    Undo a multiplication with a division.
  • $44 + 20\% \text{ of } 44 = 52.80$
    Adds $20\%$ of the sale price, but the discount was $20\%$ of the original—different base, wrong answer.

Tax works the same way in the other direction—the total is more than $100\%$ of the price:

Example

With $8\%$ sales tax included, a book costs $\$27$. What is the price before tax?

$$1.08\,p = 27$$
Total = $108\%$ of the pre-tax price
$$p = \frac{27}{1.08} = 25$$
Divide by $1.08$ — the price was $\$25$
Example

After a $25\%$ discount, a pair of headphones sells for $\$66$. What was the price before the discount?

  1. A)$\$88$
  2. B)$\$82.50$
  3. C)$\$49.50$
  4. D)$\$91$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: $0.75p = 66$, so $p = 66 \div 0.75 = 88$. Forward check: $88 \times 0.75 = 66$. ✓
  • Choice B: $66 \times 1.25 = 82.50$ adds $25\%$ of the sale price—the discount's base was the original.
  • Choice C: $66 \times 0.75 = 49.50$ discounts the discounted price again—running the change forward instead of backward.
  • Choice D: $66 + 25 = 91$ treats the percent as dollars.
Undo with division, never by adding the percent back. A $20\%$ drop is not reversed by a $20\%$ rise—the two changes use different bases.
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisPercentages: Successive Changes & Comparisons

Successive Changes & Comparisons

Two percent changes in a row do not add—each acts on a new base. Convert both to multipliers and multiply:

Example

A stock worth $\$200$ gains $10\%$ one month, then loses $10\%$ the next. Is it back to $\$200$?

$$200 \times 1.10 = 220$$
Up $10\%$ — the base was $200$
$$220 \times 0.90 = 198$$
Down $10\%$ — but the base is now $220$
$$1.10 \times 0.90 = 0.99$$
Net multiplier: a $1\%$ loss, not zero

The $10\%$ drop was measured on the larger number, so it removed more dollars than the gain added. Symmetric percents, asymmetric bases.

Example

A town's population increased by $20\%$ during one decade and by $50\%$ during the next. By what percent did the population increase over the two decades combined?

  1. A)$80\%$
  2. B)$70\%$
  3. C)$180\%$
  4. D)$35\%$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Multipliers: $1.20 \times 1.50 = 1.80$—the population is $180\%$ of the original, an increase of $80\%$.
  • Choice B: $70\%$ adds the percents ($20 + 50$). The second change grew an already-grown base, so the true total is more.
  • Choice C: $180\%$ reads the multiplier $1.80$ as the increase. Being at $180\%$ of the original means gaining $80\%$.
  • Choice D: $35\%$ averages the two rates—averaging has no meaning for successive changes.

“Percent More Than” Sets the Base

“$A$ is $20\%$ more than $B$” translates as $A = 1.20\,B$—the thing after than is the base. It does not follow that $B$ is $20\%$ less than $A$: $B = \frac{A}{1.2} \approx 0.833A$, about $16.7\%$ less.

For any successive-change question, skip the running totals: build each multiplier, multiply them once, and read the net change from the final multiplier ($0.99 \to$ down $1\%$; $1.80 \to$ up $80\%$).
Problem-Solving and Data AnalysisPercentages: Practice

Practice

Before each answer: which slot is missing—part, percent, or whole? Forward change or backward? One change or two?

Practice
  1. 1.

    Of the $48$ students in a homeroom, $12$ walk to school. What percent of the students walk to school?

    1. A)$25\%$
    2. B)$36\%$
    3. C)$4\%$
    4. D)$2.5\%$

    The correct answer is A. The percent slot is missing: $\frac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \frac{12}{48} = \frac{1}{4} = 25\%$.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $12 \div 48 = 0.25$. Anchor check: $12$ is a quarter of $48$. ✓
    • Choice B: $36$ is $48 - 12$—the students who don't walk, and not a percent of anything.
    • Choice C: $4$ divides the wrong way ($48 \div 12$): that says the whole is $4$ times the part, not what percent the part is.
    • Choice D: $2.5\%$ is the right division with a lost decimal place: $0.25$ is $25\%$, not $2.5\%$.
  2. 2.

    A $\$120$ jacket is on sale for $35\%$ off. What is the sale price?

    1. A)$\$78$
    2. B)$\$42$
    3. C)$\$85$
    4. D)$\$162$

    The correct answer is A. One multiplier: $35\%$ off means keeping $65\%$.

    $$120 \times 0.65 = 78$$
    Keep $100\% - 35\% = 65\%$
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Or the long way: discount $= 0.35 \times 120 = 42$, and $120 - 42 = 78$. ✓
    • Choice B: $\$42$ is the discount, not the price you pay—the most common one-step-short answer.
    • Choice C: $\$85$ subtracts $35$ dollars; the discount is $35$ percent.
    • Choice D: $\$162$ multiplies by $1.35$—a markup instead of a markdown.
  3. 3.

    Including $8\%$ sales tax, Marcus paid $\$648$ for a laptop. What was the price of the laptop before tax?

    1. A)$\$596.16$
    2. B)$\$600$
    3. C)$\$640$
    4. D)$\$699.84$

    The correct answer is B. The total is $108\%$ of the pre-tax price—divide to go backward:

    $$1.08\,p = 648$$
    Price plus $8\%$ tax
    $$p = \frac{648}{1.08} = 600$$
    Forward check: $600 \times 1.08 = 648$ ✓
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $648 \times 0.92$ removes $8\%$ of the total—but the tax was $8\%$ of the smaller pre-tax price. Wrong base.
    • Choice B: Undo a multiplication with a division. ✓
    • Choice C: $\$640$ subtracts $8$ dollars, not $8$ percent.
    • Choice D: $648 \times 1.08$ adds tax again—running the change forward from the wrong starting point.
  4. 4.

    A store raises the price of a gadget by $25\%$, then offers a $20\%$ discount on the new price. How does the final price compare with the original price?

    1. A)It is equal to the original price
    2. B)It is $5\%$ higher
    3. C)It is $5\%$ lower
    4. D)It is $45\%$ higher

    The correct answer is A. Multipliers, multiplied:

    $$1.25 \times 0.80 = 1.00$$
    Up $25\%$, then down $20\%$ of the raised price
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Net multiplier exactly $1$: the $20\%$ cut acted on a larger base, so it removed the entire $25\%$ gain. (Try $\$100$: $\to \$125 \to \$100$.)
    • Choice B: $+5\%$ comes from adding $+25 - 20$—successive percents never add.
    • Choice C: $-5\%$ is the same addition error with the sign guessed the other way.
    • Choice D: $+45\%$ adds the sizes of both changes as if both pushed the price up.

Things to Remember

  • Part $=$ percent $\times$ whole. Find which slot is missing; the number next to “of” is the whole.
  • Convert percents to decimals before computing—and watch the decimal point ($4\% = 0.04$).
  • Percent change is one multiplier: $\times(1+r)$ up, $\times(1-r)$ down. Percent change $= \frac{\text{change}}{\text{original}}$—divide by the before value.
  • To find the value before a change, divide by the multiplier. Adding the percent back uses the wrong base—always.
  • Successive changes multiply: $+20\%$ then $+50\%$ is $1.2 \times 1.5 = 1.8$, an $80\%$ gain. Read the net change off the final multiplier.
  • “$A$ is $r\%$ more than $B$” → $A = (1+r)B$; the base is whatever follows than.
  • Anchor-check every answer with $10\%$ / $50\%$ estimates.
