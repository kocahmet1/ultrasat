Area and Volume

Area counts the square units inside a flat figure; volume counts the cubic units inside a solid. The SAT provides a reference sheet with every formula—but the students who know the short list below solve these questions in half the time.

FigureAreaPerimeter / Circumference
Rectangle$A = \ell w$$P = 2\ell + 2w$
Triangle$A = \frac{1}{2}bh$sum of the sides
Circle$A = \pi r^2$$C = 2\pi r$
SolidVolume
Prism or cylinder (uniform cross-section)$V = \textcolor{#0f6cbd}{B}h$ — area of the base $\times$ height
Rectangular box$V = \ell w h$
Cylinder$V = \pi r^2 h$
Cone$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$
Pyramid$V = \frac{1}{3}\ell w h$
Sphere$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$
A cone is one-third of its cylinder; a pyramid is one-third of its box.

Strategies

  • Height means perpendicular height—the right-angle distance to the base, never a slanted side.
  • Composite figures: split into pieces you know, then add—or take a big figure and subtract the holes.
  • Working backward: plug in what's known and solve the formula for the missing dimension.
  • Scaling: multiply every length by $k$ → perimeter $\times k$, area $\times k^2$, volume $\times k^3$.
  • Units are a free check: area answers in units$^2$, volume in units$^3$.
Given a diameter? Halve it before anything else—the circle formulas run on the radius, and the diameter-as-radius slip is this domain's most common wrong answer.

Area of Common Figures

The Height Is Perpendicular — Always

In $A = \frac{1}{2}bh$, the height is the right-angle distance from the base to the opposite vertex. Test writers love to hand you a slanted side you don't need:

Example

What is the area of the triangle shown?

  1. A)$42$
  2. B)$54$
  3. C)$84$
  4. D)$108$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: $A = \frac{1}{2}(12)(7) = 42$. The $7$ is the dashed perpendicular height—the right-angle mark says so.
  • Choice B: $\frac{1}{2}(12)(9) = 54$ uses the slanted side $9$ as the height. Slants are longer than heights—and never in the formula.
  • Choice C: $12 \times 7 = 84$ forgets the $\frac{1}{2}$—that's the area of the surrounding rectangle.
  • Choice D: $12 \times 9 = 108$: both mistakes at once.
Example

A circle has a diameter of $12$ centimeters. What is its area?

$$r = \frac{12}{2} = 6$$
Diameter to radius first
$$A = \pi (6)^2 = 36\pi \approx 113.1 \text{ cm}^2$$
$\pi r^2$ — with the radius, not the diameter

Using $12$ directly would give $144\pi$—four times too big, since the radius got squared.

Two reflexes worth training: see a slanted side → ask “is this the height?”; see a diameter → halve it before it touches a formula.

Composite Figures

Odd-shaped figures are ordinary figures in disguise. Split and add, or enclose and subtract—both work, so use one to solve and the other to check.

Example

What is the area of the figure? Two routes:

Enclose and subtract
$$10 \times 8 = 80$$
The full rectangle
$$80 - (4 \times 3) = 68$$
Remove the notch
Split and add
$$10 \times 5 = 50$$
Bottom rectangle ($8 - 3 = 5$ tall)
$$50 + (6 \times 3) = 68$$
Plus the top-left piece ($10 - 4 = 6$ wide)

Same $68$ square units both ways—agreement is the check.

Example
$$A_{\text{rect}} = 4 \times 6 = 24$$
The rectangle
$$A_{\text{semi}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi(2)^2 = 2\pi$$
Half a circle of radius $2$ — half the width
$$A = 24 + 2\pi \approx 30.3$$
Add the pieces
Shaded-region questions are subtraction in costume: whole figure $-$ unshaded part. Find the two easy areas; the hard-looking one is their difference.
Geometry and TrigonometryArea and Volume: Volume

Volume

Rule: Any solid with the same cross-section all the way up—prism, cylinder—has volume $V = Bh$: the area of the base times the height. The box ($\ell w \cdot h$) and cylinder ($\pi r^2 \cdot h$) formulas are just this rule with the base filled in.

Example
$$B = \pi(3)^2 = 9\pi$$
Area of the circular base
$$V = Bh = 9\pi \times 10 = 90\pi \approx 282.7$$
Base area times height

Working Backward

Example

A rectangular storage box has a volume of $240$ cubic feet. Its base is $8$ feet long and $5$ feet wide. What is the height of the box?

$$240 = 8 \times 5 \times h$$
Fill the formula with what's known
$$h = \frac{240}{40} = 6 \text{ ft}$$
Divide by the base area

The One-Third Family

A cone is $\frac{1}{3}$ of the cylinder that shares its base and height; a pyramid is $\frac{1}{3}$ of the matching box. One fraction, remembered once, prices all four solids.

Example

A cylinder and a cone have the same radius and the same height. The volume of the cylinder is $54$ cubic inches. What is the volume of the cone?

  1. A)$18$
  2. B)$54$
  3. C)$27$
  4. D)$162$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Cone $= \frac{1}{3} \times$ cylinder $= \frac{54}{3} = 18$ cubic inches.
  • Choice B: Equal only if the $\frac{1}{3}$ never existed—the cone tapers to a point and loses two-thirds of the space.
  • Choice C: $27$ halves the cylinder—the factor is a third, not a half.
  • Choice D: $162$ multiplies by $3$—the relationship run in the wrong direction.

Scaling: What Doubling Really Does

Scale every length of a figure by the same factor $k$, and the measures grow by their dimension:

MeasureDimensionMultiplied by
Perimeter, circumference (lengths)$1$D$k$
Area, surface area$2$D$k^2$
Volume$3$D$k^3$
Example

The side doubled, but area is two-dimensional—it picks up the factor once per dimension: $2 \times 2 = 4$. Volume, with three dimensions, would pick it up three times: $2^3 = 8$.

Example

The radius of a sphere is tripled. The volume of the new sphere is how many times the volume of the original?

  1. A)$27$
  2. B)$3$
  3. C)$9$
  4. D)$81$
Process of Elimination:
  • Choice A: Volume is $3$-dimensional: $k^3 = 3^3 = 27$. (Or watch it in the formula: $\frac{4}{3}\pi(3r)^3 = 27 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.)
  • Choice B: $3$ is the length factor—volume compounds it across all three dimensions.
  • Choice C: $9 = 3^2$ is the surface area factor—one dimension short.
  • Choice D: $81 = 3^4$—one dimension too many.
The exponent on $k$ always equals the exponent in the units: cm → $k$, cm$^2$ → $k^2$, cm$^3$ → $k^3$. Units and scaling are the same fact.
Geometry and TrigonometryArea and Volume: Practice

Practice

Before computing: Is the height perpendicular? Is that a radius or a diameter? Add pieces or subtract holes? And what dimension is the question living in?

Practice
  1. 1.

    What is the area of the triangle shown?

    1. A)$30$
    2. B)$40$
    3. C)$60$
    4. D)$24$

    The correct answer is A. $A = \frac{1}{2}bh = \frac{1}{2}(10)(6) = 30$. The $8$ is a slanted side—scenery, not an ingredient.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Base $10$, perpendicular height $6$ (the one with the right-angle mark). ✓
    • Choice B: $\frac{1}{2}(10)(8) = 40$ promotes the slant to a height.
    • Choice C: $10 \times 6 = 60$ skips the $\frac{1}{2}$.
    • Choice D: $\frac{1}{2}(8)(6) = 24$ builds the formula from the two sides that aren't a base–height pair.
  2. 2.

    A rectangular patio measures $12$ feet by $9$ feet. A square flower bed with sides of $3$ feet is built into one corner, and the rest of the patio is paved. What is the paved area, in square feet?

    1. A)$99$
    2. B)$108$
    3. C)$105$
    4. D)$117$

    The correct answer is A. Enclose and subtract:

    $$12 \times 9 = 108$$
    The whole patio
    $$108 - 3^2 = 99$$
    Remove the $3 \times 3$ flower bed
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: Whole minus hole. ✓
    • Choice B: $108$ paves right over the flower bed—the subtraction never happened.
    • Choice C: $105$ subtracts $3$—the bed's side length—instead of its $9$-square-foot area.
    • Choice D: $117$ adds the bed's area; holes subtract.
  3. 3.

    A cylinder has a volume of $72\pi$ cubic centimeters and a radius of $3$ centimeters. What is the height of the cylinder, in centimeters?

    1. A)$2$
    2. B)$8$
    3. C)$12$
    4. D)$24$

    The correct answer is B.

    $$72\pi = \pi(3)^2 h$$
    $V = \pi r^2 h$ with the knowns filled in
    $$h = \frac{72\pi}{9\pi} = 8$$
    Divide by the base area $9\pi$
    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $2$ divides by $36$—that's using $6$ (the diameter) as the radius.
    • Choice B: Check: $\pi(9)(8) = 72\pi$. ✓
    • Choice C: $12$ divides $72$ by $6$—by the diameter itself, not the base area.
    • Choice D: $24$ divides $72$ by $3$—by the radius unsquared.
  4. 4.

    Every dimension of a rectangular box is doubled. The volume of the new box is how many times the volume of the original box?

    1. A)$8$
    2. B)$2$
    3. C)$4$
    4. D)$6$

    The correct answer is A. Volume lives in three dimensions, and each one doubles: $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$.

    Process of Elimination:
    • Choice A: $V = (2\ell)(2w)(2h) = 8\,\ell wh$. ✓
    • Choice B: $2$ is the length factor—only one dimension's worth.
    • Choice C: $4 = 2^2$ is the area factor—faces quadruple; the inside octuples.
    • Choice D: $6$ adds the three doublings ($2+2+2$); scaling factors multiply.

Things to Remember

  • Rectangle $\ell w$ · triangle $\frac{1}{2}bh$ · circle $\pi r^2$, circumference $2\pi r$. Height is perpendicular—ignore slanted sides.
  • See a diameter? Halve it before it enters any formula.
  • Composite figures: split and add, or enclose and subtract—do both to check. Shaded region $=$ whole $-$ unshaded.
  • $V = Bh$ for prisms and cylinders; cones and pyramids are $\frac{1}{3}$ of their full-height partners; sphere $= \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$.
  • Backward problems: fill the formula with knowns, solve for the missing dimension.
  • Scale lengths by $k$: perimeter $\times k$, area $\times k^2$, volume $\times k^3$—the exponent matches the units.
