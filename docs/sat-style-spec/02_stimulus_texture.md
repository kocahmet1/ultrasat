## Sentence architecture

- **Conventions items are 1–2 sentences at ~24–27 words per sentence.** They are long single
  sentences, not several short ones. This is the signature: one architecturally complex sentence
  containing the blank.
- **Transitions are 2.7 sentences / ~20 words per sentence** — two set-up sentences, then a third
  opening with the blank (83% of items put the blank at the start of the final sentence).
- **Rhetorical Synthesis is 6.1 "sentences" / 15 words each** — that is the bullet structure:
  ~5 bullets averaging 14 words (median 14, p10 8, p90 20).
- **Command of Evidence has the longest sentences of any type (32 words)** — dense claim-plus-
  qualification prose.

Read this as: **em dashes and colons are common (roughly one every 270 words); semicolons are
rare in stimulus prose (one every ~930 words)** and appear mostly as *options* in Boundaries items,
not as authorial style. Parentheses are used mainly for glosses — a defined term, a date range,
a scientific name, a unit — not for asides.

## Subject-matter and framing conventions

- **6% of all stimuli carry the literary preamble** `The following text is [adapted] from
  <AUTHOR>'s <YEAR> <novel|short story|poem|play> <TITLE>.` These land almost exclusively in
  Text Structure and Purpose (27), Words in Context (26), and Central Ideas and Details (18) —
  i.e. **literary passages never appear in Conventions, Transitions, or Rhetorical Synthesis.**
- Verbatim preamble patterns observed:
  - `The following text is from Jane Austen's 1811 novel *Sense and Sensibility*.`
  - `The following text is adapted from Johanna Spyri's 1881 novel *Heidi* (translated by Elisabeth Stork in 1915).`
  - `The following text is adapted from María Cristina Mena's 1914 short story "The Vine-Leaf."`
  - `The following text is adapted from Countee Cullen's 1926 poem "Thoughts in a Zoo."`
  - A one-sentence orienting gloss may follow, e.g. *"An elderly shop owner is looking at a
    picture that he recently acquired and hopes to sell."* or *"A cypress is a type of evergreen tree."*
- **22% of stimuli invoke research framing** (`researcher`, `scientist`, `scholar`, `study`,
  `team`, `colleagues`). The canonical opener is
  `<Full Name> and colleagues <verb-past> ...` or `In her <YEAR> article "<Title>," scholar <Name> ...`
  or `Research conducted by <field> <Name> suggests that ...`
- Named researchers skew deliberately international: Marta Coll, Carlo Bianchi, Carla Morri,
  Jean-Bernard Caron, Katarina Miljkovic, María Cristina Mena, Countee Cullen. **Invent names in
  the same register — non-Anglo naming traditions, plausible for the field and region.**
- Topic mix per module should span: natural science (astro/bio/earth/materials), social science
  (archaeology, econ, psych, linguistics), humanities/art history, history/primary-source, and
  1–2 literary passages. Indigenous and Global South subject matter is routine, treated as
  scholarship rather than as exotica.

## Parenthetical gloss convention

CB glosses any term a 15-year-old may not know, inline and without condescension:
`transgenic`, `mimesis`, `ephemera`, `the Burgess Shale, a site in the Canadian Rockies that is
rich in fossils`, `logging (cutting down trees for commercial and other uses)`. Use this device —
it is a strong style marker and it lets you write about genuinely specialized topics.
