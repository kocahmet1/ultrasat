# College Board Digital SAT — Reading & Writing Item Style Spec
### Derived from 1,050 labeled Question-Bank items + 4 official practice forms (PT2, PT3, PT4, PT5)
### Binding authoring contract for ULTRASAT Practice Test 4, R&W Modules 1–2

---

## 0. THE ESSENCE (read this twice)

College Board R&W items are **not vocabulary/grammar trivia**. Every item is a
*controlled inference from a self-contained text*. The measured construct is:

> Can the student hold a short, information-dense passage in working memory, identify the
> single logical relationship the passage actually asserts, and reject four plausible-sounding
> statements that are each true-ish, or textually adjacent, but not the thing the passage licenses?

Six invariants that separate real CB items from Barron's/Princeton Review imitations:

1. **Self-containment.** The passage supplies 100% of what's needed. No outside knowledge,
   ever. If a student needs to know what a "mycorrhizal network" is, the passage defines it
   in-line, usually with an em-dash appositive or a "that is" gloss.
2. **The passage constrains, it doesn't decorate.** Every clause in a CB stimulus is doing
   work: setting up a contrast, licensing an inference, or planting a distractor trap. There
   is *no* scene-setting filler. Prep-company passages ramble; CB passages are compressed to
   the point of being slightly dry.
3. **Distractors are wrong for *specifiable* reasons, not for being silly.** The rationale
   for every wrong answer in the official bank is one of a small set of formulas (§7). If you
   can't write the one-sentence formula for why a distractor fails, the distractor is bad.
4. **The trap is almost always over-reach or scope drift**, not factual error. The classic CB
   distractor is *supported by one sentence of the passage but not by the passage as a whole*,
   or is *stronger than the passage's hedged claim* ("proves" vs. "suggests"), or *reverses
   which party holds which view*.
5. **Hedged scientific register.** CB writes "suggests," "may," "is consistent with,"
   "researchers hypothesize." Confident claims appear only where the distractor's error IS
   the overconfidence.
6. **Short.** Median stimulus is 40–95 words depending on skill (§2). Prep companies write
   150-word passages; CB almost never exceeds 150 and rarely exceeds 120 outside Cross-Text.

**Ordering (hard rule, mirrors the operational form):** items are grouped by domain in the

Ideas. Within Craft & Structure the skill order is WiC → TSP → CTC; within Information & Ideas
it is CID → COE → INF; within Expression of Ideas it is Transitions → Rhetorical Synthesis.
Within each *non-Conventions skill*, difficulty is non-decreasing. Conventions items are
ordered easier→harder **across the whole domain**, interleaving Boundaries and Form/Structure.

**Difficulty mix:** Module 1 = 4 easy / 17 medium / 6 hard. Module 2 = 5 easy / 15 medium /
7 hard. (≈17% easy, ≈59% medium, ≈24% hard — Module 2 is marginally harder, matching the
published linear forms.)

## 3. STEM WORDING (verbatim; these are fixed strings, do not paraphrase)

| Skill | Stem | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Words in Context | `Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?` | 100% |
| Transitions | `Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?` | 100% |
| Boundaries / Form-Structure-Sense | `Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?` | 100% |
| Inferences | `Which choice most logically completes the text?` | 100% |
| Text Structure & Purpose | `Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?` (32%) / `Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?` (23%) / `Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?` (40%) | — |
| Central Ideas & Details | `Which choice best states the main idea of the text?` (41%) / `According to the text, …?` (22%) / `Based on the text, what can be concluded about …?` (19%) | — |
| Cross-Text Connections | `Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the underlined claim in Text 1?` (42%) / `Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which statement?` (26%) | — |
| Command of Evidence (quant) | `Which choice most effectively uses data from the table/graph to complete the text?` (or `…the statement?` / `…the example?`) | 41% of COE |
| Command of Evidence (textual) | `Which finding, if true, would most directly support/weaken the researchers' hypothesis?` / `Which quotation from [work] most effectively illustrates the claim?` | 27% / 20% |
| Rhetorical Synthesis | `While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:` + 5 bullets + `The student wants to <goal>. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?` | 100% |

Preamble for literary/source-attributed passages: `The following text is from …` /
`The following text is adapted from …` — used in ~22% of Reading items (WiC 16%, TSP 28%,
CID 22%). **ULTRASAT house rule (continued from Exam 3): all such passages are original and
must be labeled honestly** — `The following text is from an original short story.`,
`The following text is from an original novel.`,
`The following original poem was written for this practice test.`, or, for CTC/COE items about
invented works, an in-text signal such as `a literary critic discussing the fictional novelist …`.

## 4. SKILL-BY-SKILL DESIGN RULES

### 4.1 Words in Context (8 items)
- Exactly one blank `______` (6+ underscores). Blank sits in the **second half** of the passage in ~80% of items.
- Options: **82% single words, 18% short phrases** (`adapt to`, `receptive to`, `a catalyst of`). Across our 8 items: 6–7 single-word sets, 1–2 phrase sets. All four options must be the **same part of speech and same grammatical shape**.
- The passage must contain an explicit **semantic constraint** — a contrast signal (`however`, `although`, `by contrast`), a causal signal (`because`, `since`, `thus`), or an appositive gloss — that makes exactly one option defensible. The correct word is never merely "the nicest fit"; it is *entailed*.
- Difficulty ladder: **Easy** = high-frequency word, one obvious contrast (`collected`, `reflect`, `widespread`). **Medium** = mid-frequency, requires tracking a two-step relation (`conform to`, `atypical`, `integral`). **Hard** = academic/abstract register where 2+ options are defensible on a shallow read (`tenuous`, `latent`, `notional`, `repudiates`, `corroborate`).
- **Trap design:** at least one distractor must be a word that fits the *topic* but not the *logical slot* (topic-associate trap); at least one must be a near-synonym of the key that is too strong or too weak.
- Never use a word in the passage that gives the answer away by morphological echo.

### 4.2 Text Structure & Purpose (4 items)
- Three sub-forms; across our 4 items use: 2× `function of the underlined sentence`, 1× `main purpose`, 1× `overall structure`.
- For the underlined-function form, mark the span with `[UNDERLINED] … [/UNDERLINED]` around **exactly one sentence**, and the sentence must have a *structural* role (concession, pivot, illustration, qualification) — never the thesis itself.
- Correct answers are phrased as **abstract rhetorical descriptions** ("It introduces a limitation that the rest of the text goes on to address"), never as content summaries.
- **Trap design:** the top distractor is a *content-accurate summary* of the underlined portion that misdescribes its function. Another distractor names a real rhetorical move that occurs *elsewhere* in the text.
- For `overall structure`, answers are two- or three-part templates: "It begins by …, then …, and concludes by …".

### 4.3 Cross-Text Connections (2 items)
- Format: `Text 1` (≈65–75 w) then blank line then `Text 2` (≈60–75 w). Both labels must appear literally.
- Text 2 must **respond to, qualify, or complicate** Text 1 — not simply contradict it. The most CB-like relationship is: Text 1 states an explanation; Text 2 accepts the observation but supplies a different mechanism, or accepts the mechanism but limits its scope.
- One of our two items uses the `how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the underlined claim in Text 1` form (mark the claim with `[UNDERLINED]…[/UNDERLINED]`); the other uses `both authors would most likely agree with which statement?`.
- **Trap design:** distractors that (a) state Text 1's view back to you, (b) state a view Text 2 holds but that is irrelevant to the specific claim asked about, (c) overstate Text 2's disagreement into wholesale rejection.

### 4.4 Central Ideas & Details (4 items)
- Across our 4: 2× `main idea`, 1× `According to the text, …`, 1× `Based on the text, what can be concluded about …`.
- Main-idea keys are **whole-text** claims. The #1 distractor is a true statement drawn from a single sentence — the "true but partial" trap. Author it deliberately.
- ~22% of CID items use an original literary passage: use exactly one across our four.

### 4.5 Command of Evidence (8 items) — the biggest block, and the biggest tell
Sub-forms and our allocation:
- **Quantitative (4 of 8; CB share 41%).** Prose describes a study, then a sentence to complete. The graphic is a **table (2), bar graph (1), or line graph (1)**. Options are data statements; three are false *against the graphic* or true-but-irrelevant to the claim. Golden rule: the correct option must be *both* accurate per the graphic *and* the datum that actually supports the stated claim. The classic trap is a **true reading of the graphic that doesn't serve the claim**.
- **Hypothesis support/weaken (2 of 8; CB share 27%).** `Which finding, if true, would most directly support/weaken …?` The passage states a hypothesis plus an observation. Distractors: consistent-but-neutral findings, findings that support a *different* hypothesis in the passage, findings that restate the observation.
- **Literary quotation (2 of 8; CB share 20%).** A claim about an (original) literary work, then four quotations. Only one *illustrates* the claim; others illustrate adjacent claims about the same work.
- Retention rule: quantitative items must be readable without the graphic's absence being fatal — the prose names the variables.

### 4.6 Inferences (2 items)
- Passage ends mid-sentence with `______` at the very end, followed by the stem `Which choice most logically completes the text?`
- The blank completes a sentence that begins with a logical connective already in the text — `suggesting that ______`, `this indicates that ______`, `therefore, ______`. The inference must be **deductively forced** by the passage, not merely plausible.
- **Trap design:** the strongest distractor is a claim that is *plausible in the world* but requires a premise the passage never supplies. A second distractor reverses the direction of the inferred relation.

### 4.7 Boundaries (8 items) — punctuation only
Conventions coverage across the form (each rule used **once**, matching the bank's frequency profile):
| # | Rule | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sentence boundary: two main clauses joined without a coordinator (period/semicolon vs. comma splice) | Easy |
| 2 | Coordination of main clauses: comma + coordinating conjunction | Easy |
| 3 | Supplementary element set off with **paired commas** (nonrestrictive appositive) | Medium |
| 4 | Introductory subordinate clause / phrase followed by main clause | Medium |
| 5 | **No** punctuation between subject and verb, or between verb and its complement | Medium |
| 6 | Supplementary element set off with **paired dashes** | Medium |
| 7 | **Colon** introducing an explanation/list after a complete main clause | Hard |
| 8 | **Complex series**: semicolons separating items that contain internal commas | Hard |
- Passage is 1–2 sentences, 32–56 words. The blank is `______` inside the sentence and the four options are the same word(s) with different punctuation (`writers` / `writers,` / `writers —` / `writers;`).
- Hard items achieve difficulty by making the *clause structure* hard to see (long noun phrase before the verb, an embedded appositive, a parenthetical between subject and predicate), never by exotic punctuation.

### 4.8 Form, Structure, and Sense (7 items) — grammar only
| # | Rule | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Subject–verb agreement with an intervening prepositional phrase / relative clause | Medium |
| 2 | Verb tense & aspect consistency in a sequence of events | Medium |
| 3 | Pronoun–antecedent agreement | Medium |
| 4 | Verbs expressing tense: simple past vs. present perfect | Easy |
| 5 | Finite vs. nonfinite verb form (the clause needs a finite main verb) | Medium |
| 6 | Possessive nouns / possessive determiners (`its` vs. `it's`, plural possessive) | Medium |
| 7 | Subject–modifier placement (dangling/misplaced modifier) | Hard |
- Options for agreement/tense items are inflectional variants of one verb. Options for modifier-placement items are four full clause openings.
- Passage 30–55 words, 1–2 sentences.

### 4.9 Transitions (6 items)
- **Every keyed transition in a form is distinct, and the six span at least 5 relationship categories.** Assigned keys for this form (do not deviate):
  | Position | Key | Relationship |
  |---|---|---|
  | M1 Q22 (easy) | `For instance,` | example |
  | M1 Q23 (medium) | `By contrast,` | contrast/concession |
  | M1 Q24 (medium) | `Therefore,` | cause–result |
  | M2 Q23 (medium) | `Moreover,` | addition |
  | M2 Q24 (medium) | `Likewise,` | similarity |
  | M2 Q25 (hard) | `Indeed,` | emphasis/intensification |
- The blank is **sentence-initial** and the passage is 2 sentences (occasionally 3), 44–68 words.
- **The four options must represent four different relationships.** Never offer two synonyms of the key.
- Difficulty comes from the second sentence being genuinely ambiguous on a shallow read: an easy item has an explicit contrast; a hard item requires noticing that the second sentence *intensifies* rather than merely *adds*, or *specifies* rather than *concludes*.
- Distractors are drawn from the CB house inventory: `However,` `Nevertheless,` `In addition,` `For example,` `Specifically,` `As a result,` `Consequently,` `Similarly,` `Instead,` `Meanwhile,` `In fact,` `That is,` `Finally,` `Ultimately,` `Alternatively,` `In contrast,` `Conversely,` `Thus,` `Hence,` `Still,` `Second,` `Previously,` `Of course,`.

### 4.10 Rhetorical Synthesis (5 items)
- Fixed preamble `While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:` then **exactly 5 bullets** (each bullet prefixed with `• `), then the goal sentence.
- Notes are **telegraphic sentence fragments or short declaratives** — no connectives between them. Each bullet is 8–22 words.
- Goal sentences follow CB's inventory: `emphasize the aim of the research study`, `present the study and its findings`, `emphasize a similarity/difference between X and Y`, `explain an advantage/disadvantage of X`, `introduce X to an audience already familiar with Y`, `describe X to an audience unfamiliar with Y`, `specify how X works`, `contrast X and Y`, `place X in the context of Y`. Use five *different* goals across the form.
- Options are single well-formed sentences (18–35 words) that all use information from the notes accurately. **Only the goal discriminates.** This is the crux: a Rhetorical Synthesis item where a distractor is factually wrong is a broken item. Distractors must be *accurate but off-goal* (they give findings when the goal is aim, method when the goal is result, one specimen when the goal is a comparison, etc.).
- Difficulty: **Easy** = goal is a single element present in one bullet. **Medium** = goal requires combining two bullets. **Hard** = goal is audience-calibrated ("already familiar with X" ⇒ the correct option omits the definition of X that a distractor supplies).

## 5. TOPIC / DISCIPLINE DISTRIBUTION

Target across 54 items (CB's measured profile): **Science ≈ 34%, Humanities & Arts ≈ 26%,
Social science ≈ 20%, Literature (original prose/poetry) ≈ 12%, History & civics ≈ 8%.**

CB's characteristic moves you must reproduce:
- Named researchers with **globally diverse names**, cited as `researcher-role Firstname Lastname` (`ecologist Priya Raghunathan`, `art historian Tomás Belaúnde`). Roughly 55% of Reading items name a researcher or scholar.
- Frequent representation of Indigenous, Black, Latin American, Asian, and African scholars, artists, and communities — presented as ordinary subject matter, never as the "point" of the passage.
- Studies are cited without journal names or dates in most items; a year appears in ~25%.
- Poetry items give a one-line gloss of any unfamiliar image ("A cypress is a type of evergreen tree.").

**Originality (hard constraint):** every named person, place, work, study, organism, artifact,
and dataset must be **invented for this form**. A banned-entity list of 1,992 proper nouns
appearing in the reference PDFs and in ULTRASAT Exams 1–3 is at
`outputs/used_named_entities.txt`; nothing on it may appear. No two items on the whole form
may share a topic, and no item may reuse a topic used in Exams 1–3.

## 6. TONE & SENTENCE-LEVEL REGISTER

- Third person, present tense for scholarly claims, past tense for study procedure.
- Hedges: `suggests`, `indicates`, `may`, `appears to`, `is consistent with`, `researchers hypothesize`.
- One em-dash appositive or parenthetical gloss per Reading passage on average.
- No rhetorical questions, no second person, no exclamations, no humor, no "interestingly".
- No sentence longer than ~35 words; average 18–24 words in Reading passages.
- Numerals for data; spelled-out numbers below ten in prose.
- Quotation marks: curly (`“ ”`, `’`). Em dashes: `—` with no surrounding spaces in CB's
  typesetting, but the ULTRASAT renderer uses spaced em dashes in Boundaries options.

## 7. RATIONALE / EXPLANATION FORMAT (production schema)

A single string, **≥ 120 characters**, beginning literally with `Choice X ` where X is the keyed
letter. Structure, following the official bank:

> `Choice C is correct.` + one sentence naming the mechanism ("The convention being tested is
> the punctuation of a supplementary element within a sentence." for Conventions; for Reading,
> a sentence stating what the passage establishes). + one sentence tying the key to that
> mechanism. + `Choice A (…) is incorrect because …` for each of the other three, each giving
> the **specific** failure, not "it does not fit."

Distractor-failure formulas that appear in the official rationales — use these, not free-form:
- *over-reach*: "goes beyond what the text supports"
- *partial*: "describes only one detail rather than the text as a whole"
- *reversal*: "reverses the relationship the text describes"
- *unsupported premise*: "the text never indicates that …"
- *off-goal* (Rhetorical Synthesis): "accurately uses the notes but does not accomplish the stated goal, since it presents the method rather than the aim"
- *topic-associate* (WiC): "is associated with the subject matter but does not describe …"
- *convention*: "no punctuation is needed between a subject and its verb"

## 8. MECHANICAL / SCHEMA CONSTRAINTS (validator-enforced — violations are build failures)

Each item object:
```
{
  "originalQuestionNumber": <int, 1..27 in order>,
  "passage": "<stimulus; HTML allowed only for tables/SVG>",
  "text": "<the stem, verbatim from §3>",
  "questionType": "multiple-choice",
  "options": ["…","…","…","…"],      // 4, no letter prefixes, no HTML, no duplicates
  "correctAnswer": <0..3>,
  "acceptedAnswers": null,
  "difficulty": "easy" | "medium" | "hard",
  "subcategory": "<kebab-case skill>",
  "subcategoryId": <int>,
  "explanation": "<see §7>"
}
```
- Stimulus word count must be within `[skill floor, 150]`. Floors: words-in-context 50,
  text-structure-purpose 75, cross-text-connections 100, central-ideas-details 70,
  command-of-evidence 65, inferences 65, boundaries 35, form-structure-sense 35,
  transitions 45, rhetorical-synthesis 70.
- Exactly one `______` blank in words-in-context, inferences, boundaries,
  form-structure-sense, transitions. Zero blanks elsewhere.
- `[UNDERLINED]…[/UNDERLINED]` allowed **only** in TSP underlined-function items (and, for
  this form, the Cross-Text "underlined claim" item — see §4.3; the validator will be extended
  accordingly).
- Rhetorical synthesis passages need ≥ 5 `•` bullets.
- Quantitative graphics: exactly one `<table>` **or** one `<svg>` per item, only on
  command-of-evidence items. Tables need `<th>` and `<td>`. SVGs need
  `data-graph-type="bar"|"line"`, `viewBox`, `role="img"`, a nonempty `<title>` and `<desc>`.
- Answer-letter distribution per module must be **6/7/7/7** with no 3 identical letters in a row.
  Keyed letters are pre-assigned in `ASSIGNMENTS.md` — do not change them.
- The keyed option must not be conspicuously the longest: across long-option items, the key is
  uniquely the longest by word count in ≤ 50% and by characters in ≤ 65%.
- Explanations must start `Choice <keyed letter> `.
