# College Board Digital SAT — Reading & Writing Style Spec (measured)

Derived from 1,200 official SAT Question Bank items (with official rationales) plus official
Practice Tests 2–5. Every number below is **measured**, not guessed. Author to these numbers.

---

## 0. THE ESSENCE — what CB is actually testing

The single most-copied-badly feature of the SAT R&W section is this: **CB never tests outside
knowledge, never tests "which sounds better," and never tests taste.** Every keyed answer is
forced by a *specific, quotable span of the stimulus*. Every distractor fails for a *nameable,
mechanical reason*. If a student can defend two answers, the item is broken.

Five load-bearing principles:

1. **Self-contained determinacy.** The stimulus contains the constraint that eliminates three
   choices. A well-informed adult who has never heard of the topic must still get it right.
2. **The constraint is usually one clause.** In WIC/INF/TRN, the answer is forced by a single
   "hinge" — a colon, a "because," a contrast marker, a numeric comparison, a "rather than."
   Locate the hinge before writing the choices.
3. **Distractors are *topically* attractive and *logically* dead.** They come from the semantic
   field of the passage (so they "sound right") but violate the hinge. CB's four standard
   distractor families:
   - **Contradicted** — reverses the stated relationship.
   - **Out of scope / unsupported** — true-sounding, plausible in the world, absent from the text.
   - **Half-right** — correct about part of the text, wrong about the part the stem asks for.
   - **Right relationship, wrong element** — describes something the text does say, but not the
     thing the stem points at (e.g., describes the *whole* text when asked about the *underlined*
     sentence).
4. **Tricks CB actually uses** (and what each measures):
   - *Vocabulary-field lure*: a word from the topic's register that isn't what the sentence
     asserts (measures: reading the sentence, not the topic).
   - *Polarity flip*: the reversed-direction option (measures: tracking logical direction).
   - *Scope inflation*: "all," "always," "proves," "the primary cause" (measures: resisting
     overclaim). CB keyed answers are hedged: "suggests," "may," "one factor."
   - *Data-plausible-but-unstated*: in COE-with-graphic, a choice describing a real, readable
     trend that doesn't support the claim in the sentence (measures: claim–evidence *fit*,
     not chart reading).
   - *Local-fix trap*: in Boundaries, a choice that fixes the visible comma but creates a splice
     or a fragment (measures: sentence-boundary competence, not comma feel).
   - *Notes-recombination trap*: in Rhetorical Synthesis, a fluent sentence that uses accurate
     notes but does not do the stated **goal** (measures: purpose over accuracy).
5. **Register.** Neutral, present-tense expository prose. Named researchers/artists with
   globally diverse names. No opinion, no humor, no second person, no rhetorical questions.
   Sentences are complex but never ornate. Em dashes and colons are used *purposefully*.

## 3. PER-SKILL SPECIFICATION

### 3.1 words-in-context (WIC) — stem: *"Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?"*
- Stimulus 2–4 sentences, one blank (`______`, 6+ underscores). Blank is mid-passage or in the
  final sentence, never in sentence 1.
- The text must **define the blank by its own logic**: a colon-expansion, a "rather than" pairing,
  a because-clause, or a concrete example that follows.
- Options: single words (or 2-word verb phrases like "give way to"). All four same part of speech.
- Difficulty ladder: *easy* = one common word, hinge is explicit ("It is delicate and sticks out,
  making it easy to break" → fragile). *medium* = the hinge is a contrast structure. *hard* =
  choices are near-synonyms differing on one feature (e.g., *established* vs *plausible* vs
  *provisional*), or the word is a slightly formal register item (*equivocal, tractable, salutary,
  attenuate, provisional, ancillary, tenuous*).
- **Never** test a word whose meaning must be known from outside; the sentence must supply it.

### 3.2 text-structure-purpose (TSP) — three stem variants, use the measured mix
- 28% *"Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?"*
- 20% *"Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"*
- 29% *"Which choice best describes the function of the underlined portion in the text as a whole?"*
  (or "underlined sentence"). Mark the span with `[UNDERLINED]…[/UNDERLINED]`.
- Structure options read as a **sequence**: "It presents X, then Y, and concludes with Z."
- Purpose options begin with a verb: "To explain…", "It illustrates…".
- Classic distractors: describes a *real* move the text makes but at the wrong location; describes
  the topic instead of the rhetorical move; overstates ("to refute," "to prove") what the text only
  raises.

### 3.3 cross-text-connections (CTC) — Text 1 / Text 2, ~140 words TOTAL
- Format exactly:
  `Text 1\n<60–80 words>\n\nText 2\n<60–80 words>`
- Stems (measured): *"Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to
  the underlined claim in Text 1?"* · *"Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree
  with which statement?"* · *"Based on the texts, how would [named researcher] (Text 2) most
  likely respond to the [claim/discussion] in Text 1?"*
- The relationship is **never flat disagreement**. Use: Text 2 accepts the observation but
  reassigns the cause; Text 2 narrows the scope; Text 2 supplies a mechanism Text 1 called
  mysterious; Text 2 says the evidence is compatible with a rival account.
- Distractors: a response Text 2's author would *not* be entitled to make; a response about a
  detail Text 1 didn't claim; flat contradiction when the actual relation is qualification.

### 3.4 central-ideas-details (CID)
- Dominant stem *"Which choice best states the main idea of the text?"* (~40%); the remainder are
  bespoke: *"According to the text, why…"*, *"What does the text most strongly suggest about…"*,
  *"Based on the text, what can be concluded about…"*.
- Main-idea options must all be **plausible summaries at the same altitude**. The wrong ones are
  a supporting detail elevated to thesis, a claim about a different subject in the passage, or an
  inference the text doesn't license.

### 3.5 command-of-evidence (COE) — measured split: 51% quantitative, 28% textual-claim, 20% quotation
Per module of 4 COE items, use: **2 quantitative (table/bar/line), 1 textual-claim, 1 quotation
or claim-support**. Across the form there must be at least one table, one bar graph, one line graph.
- **Quantitative**: stem *"Which choice most effectively uses data from the table/graph to complete
  the statement/text/example?"* The passage ends with a claim + `______`. The keyed choice must be
  (a) accurately read from the graphic AND (b) logically supportive of the *specific* claim.
  Build exactly the four CB distractor types: accurate-but-irrelevant, misread (swapped
  rows/columns/series), correct comparison in the wrong direction, and a comparison the graphic
  doesn't display.
  - Tables: `<table><thead><tr><th>…` with a caption sentence naming the units and years.
  - Graphs: inline `<svg role="img" viewBox="…" data-graph-type="bar|line"><title>…</title>
    <desc>…</desc>…</svg>` with visible axis labels and tick values. Must survive DOMPurify.
- **Textual-claim**: *"Which finding, if true, would most directly support/weaken the researchers'
  hypothesis?"* Options are one-sentence findings.
- **Quotation**: a literary work is introduced, a claim about it is made, and the four options are
  quotations from the (fictional, newly authored) work. The keyed quotation must *illustrate* the
  claim, not merely mention the topic.

### 3.6 inferences (INF) — stem always *"Which choice most logically completes the text?"*
- Stimulus ends mid-argument with `______`; the blank completes the final sentence.
- The keyed choice is the **only conclusion the given premises license** — and it is hedged.
- Options are ~16-word clauses. Distractors overreach ("proves that"), reverse the logic, or draw
  a conclusion about a related but unmentioned quantity.
- INF is where CB's scope-inflation trick is densest. The keyed answer usually contains a hedge
  ("may," "is likely," "at least some") that the distractors lack.

### 3.7 boundaries (BND) — stem always *"Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"*
Measured rule frequencies across 150 official items — **spread the 8 form items across ≥6 distinct
rules; never test the same rule twice in one module.**

| Rule | share |
|---|---|
| punctuation within a sentence (comma splices, misused semicolon/dash) | 16% |
| coordination of main clauses (comma + FANBOYS vs. splice) | 10% |
| punctuation *between* sentences (period/semicolon vs. splice) | 10% |
| end-of-sentence punctuation | 8% |
| supplementary element punctuation (paired commas / dashes / parentheses) | 9% |
| no punctuation between subject and verb | 3% |
| complex series (semicolons separating items containing commas) | 3% |
| titles and proper nouns | 3% |
| no punctuation between preposition and complement | 3% |
| colon introducing a list/elaboration (requires a full clause before it) | 3% |
| main clause + supplementary phrase (either order) | 5% |
| integrated (restrictive) vs. supplementary (nonrestrictive) relative clause | 3% |
| subordinate clause + main clause | 2% |
| items in a simple series | 2% |

- Options are the same words with different punctuation, e.g. `writers` / `writers,` / `writers —` /
  `writers;`. Keep the wording identical across choices; only punctuation varies.
- The correct answer is frequently **no punctuation at all** — that is the signature CB move
  students miss. Use it for at least one of the 8 form items.

### 3.8 form-structure-sense (FSS) — same stem as BND
Measured rule frequencies (150 official items) — spread the 7 form items across ≥5 rules:

| Rule | share |
|---|---|
| subject-verb agreement (esp. with intervening prepositional phrases, collective nouns, "each/one of") | 28% |
| verb forms within a sentence (finite vs. nonfinite; participles) | 19% |
| subject-modifier placement (dangling/misplaced modifiers) | 11% |
| verb tense/sequence across a sentence or paragraph | 15% |
| pronoun–antecedent agreement | 8% |
| possessive determiners & possessive vs. plural nouns (its/it's, students'/student's) | 6% |
| finite verbs required in main clause / relative clause | 5% |
| plural nouns and determiners | 3% |

- Modifier items: the four options are four different sentence openings/subjects.
- SVA items: bury the true subject behind a prepositional phrase or a relative clause; the
  distractors agree with the nearest noun.

### 3.9 transitions (TRN) — stem always *"Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?"*
- Blank at the **start of the final sentence** (CB's dominant placement), occasionally mid-sentence.
- **Every one of the 6 form items must key a different transition**, and the 6 must cover ≥4 of
  these relationship categories: cause–result · contrast/concession · example/specification ·
  addition · similarity · restatement · replacement · temporal/sequence · emphasis-correction ·
  synthesis.
- Pool discipline: the four options must represent **four different relationships** (never two
  contrast words in one item). Measured most-used option pool: *for example, similarly, however,
  nevertheless, in other words, specifically, as a result, for instance, likewise, instead,
  in addition, therefore, by contrast, thus, regardless, additionally, moreover, consequently.*
- Hard items key a less obvious relationship word (*granted, that said, to that end, in turn,
  again and again, in so doing, of course, still, fittingly*) or place the blank so that the
  relationship spans two prior sentences.

### 3.10 rhetorical-synthesis (RS)
- Format exactly:
  ```
  While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:
  • note 1
  • note 2
  • note 3
  • note 4
  • note 5           ← 5–7 bullets, each a fragment or short sentence
  The student wants to <GOAL>. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information
  from the notes to accomplish this goal?
  ```
- Measured goal families: emphasize a similarity / emphasize a difference / contrast X and Y /
  present the study and its findings / emphasize the aim of the research / explain an advantage
  (or disadvantage) of X / introduce X to an audience *already familiar with* Y / describe X to an
  audience *unfamiliar with* Y / define a term and give an example / specify how X does Y /
  place X in historical context / identify which of three items has the highest Z.
- All four options must be **grammatical, fluent, and factually consistent with the notes.** The
  three wrong ones fail the *goal*: they emphasize the wrong element, present a difference when a
  similarity was requested, over-explain to an audience described as already familiar, or bury the
  requested element in a subordinate clause.
- The audience-awareness goals are the highest-yield hard variant: if the audience "is already
  familiar with the Atlantic Monthly," the keyed sentence must *not* stop to explain what it is.

## 4. TOPIC DISTRIBUTION (measured, per 27-question module)

Roughly: **9–10 natural science** (ecology, astronomy, materials, geology, biology),
**6–7 social science** (archaeology, linguistics, economics, psychology, urban studies),
**5–6 humanities/arts** (visual art, music, textiles, architecture, film),
**4–5 literature/narrative** (fictional excerpts, poetry, memoir),
**2–3 history/civics** (primary-source-flavored passages).

Literature passages appear mainly in WIC, TSP, CID and the COE quotation item. Use the CB framing
*"The following text is from the 19XX story '____' by [invented author]."*

**Diversity requirement (CB does this deliberately and consistently):** named scholars, artists and
writers should be globally diverse — Nigerian, Korean, Basque, Quechua, Tamil, Finnish, Lebanese,
Māori, Brazilian, Kazakh, etc. Indigenous North American communities appear regularly and are
always named specifically and respectfully (e.g., "a Kanien'kehá:ka (Mohawk) writer").

## 5. EXPLANATION TEMPLATE (mirrors the official rationale voice)

One paragraph, 150–320 words, plain text (no markdown, no bullets):

> Choice **{letter}** ({short quote of the option}) is correct. {One sentence naming the hinge in
> the stimulus, quoting it.} {One sentence showing why the keyed option satisfies the hinge.}
> Choice {L2} is incorrect because {mechanical reason}. Choice {L3} is incorrect because {…}.
> Choice {L4} is incorrect because {…}.

Rules:
- Must literally begin `Choice {KeyedLetter}` (validator-enforced).
- Every distractor rebuttal names a *mechanism*: "is contradicted by the text," "is not supported —
  the text never states," "describes the whole text rather than the underlined sentence,"
  "signals contrast, but the second sentence extends rather than opposes the first,"
  "creates a comma splice between two main clauses."
- Never say "sounds awkward," "is less precise," or "is not the best choice."
- For Conventions items, name the convention explicitly: *"The convention being tested is
  subject-verb agreement."*

## 6. HARD PROHIBITIONS

- No reuse of any topic, researcher name, artwork, study, organism, place, or phrase from the
  official Question Bank, official Practice Tests 1–10, or this repo's Practice Tests 3 and 4.
  See `AVOID_NAMES.txt` for the PT3/PT4 name list.
- No real living people; no real named studies or datasets. Invent plausible researchers and works.
- No trick that depends on outside knowledge, ambiguity, or "best of several defensible answers."
- No option that is defensible on a fair reading. Each item must have exactly one answer that a
  panel of English teachers would agree on unanimously.
- No "all of the above"/"none of the above," no negative stems ("which is NOT"), no lettered
  prefixes inside option strings, no HTML inside options.
