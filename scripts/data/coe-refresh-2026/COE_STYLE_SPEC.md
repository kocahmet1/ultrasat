# Command of Evidence — Authoring Spec (measured from 226 official College Board items)

Source: `questionbank-export-2026-8-5 (11).pdf` — 226 official SAT Question Bank items, skill =
Command of Evidence, with official rationales (56 Easy · 69 Medium · 101 Hard). Every number
below is measured from that export. Companion to `wic-refresh-2026/WIC_STYLE_SPEC.md`.

---

## 0. The three families — and the single fact that matters most

| | Quantitative | Textual: Finding | Textual: Quotation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of bank | **111 / 226 (49%)** | **62 / 226 (27%)** | **53 / 226 (23%)** |
| Stimulus carries | a table or a bar/line graph | prose only | prose only |
| Stem | *Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?* / *Which choice best describes data from the graph that support the researchers' conclusion?* | *Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?* | *Which quotation from ["Title"] most effectively illustrates the claim?* |
| Options are | data readouts, or data + interpretation | invented experimental results | quoted passages (literary) or invented expert quotations (sourced) |
| Ends in `______` | 71 / 111 | **0 / 62** | 32 / 53 (all literary) |

**Half of Command of Evidence is a data-reading test.** Third-party banks under-build this
family badly — they write 10–20% quantitative and mostly at the "read one cell" level. The
official bank is 49% quantitative and its *hard* quantitative items contain **zero** direct
lookups.

### The essence — what is actually being measured

Every CoE item, in all three families, asks one question:

> **Does this piece of evidence do the specific job that this specific claim requires?**

Not "is it true." Not "is it about the topic." *Does it license this claim.* The student is
given a claim with a determinate logical shape and four candidate pieces of evidence, and must
check each against that shape. The measurement is **the ability to hold a claim's exact scope in
working memory while reading**.

Three consequences that must hold in every authored item:

1. **The claim must have a hard edge.** It must be possible to write a distractor that is true,
   on-topic, and still fails — and to say in one sentence *why*. If the only reason a distractor
   loses is that it is less good, the item is broken.
2. **The key must be the unique evidence of its shape.** If two options have the same logical
   relation to the claim, the item is broken, no matter how much better one reads.
3. **Nothing outside the text may be needed.** All four options are equally plausible as
   facts about the world. The item is decided entirely by fit to the stated claim.

## 1. Measured numbers

### Stimulus length (words, excluding the stem sentence)

| Family | Easy | Medium | Hard | **author to** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Textual: Finding | 75 (med 70) | 91 (med 92) | 102 (med 106) | **E 62–90 · M 78–105 · H 90–118** |
| Textual: Quotation — literary | 31 (med 27) | 37 (med 36) | 39 (med 38) | **E 22–42 · M 28–50 · H 30–52** |
| Textual: Quotation — sourced | 48 | 74 | 95 | **E 42–58 · M 62–88 · H 82–112** |
| Quantitative (prose only) | 55 (med 52) | 72 (med 70) | 88 (med 90) | **E 45–68 · M 62–85 · H 78–105** |

**Unlike Words in Context, CoE manufactures difficulty partly with length.** Finding items grow
36% from Easy to Hard; sourced-quotation items *double*. This is real and must be reproduced.
Literary-quotation stimuli are the exception — they barely grow, because their difficulty lives
entirely in the options.

Stem length is invariant: **11–15 words, mean 14**, in all families and all difficulties.

### Option length (words per option)

| Family | Easy | Medium | Hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quantitative | **7** (median 3) | 18 | 24 |
| Textual: Finding | 18 | 25 | 26 |
| Textual: Quotation | 23 | 25 | **35** |

Easy quantitative options are often **3–5 words** — bare readouts (`A. Alcedo.` `B. Pacaya.`
`C. Fernandina.` `D. Maule.`). Hard quantitative options are **20–36 word** compound sentences.
This 5× swing is the loudest single signal of quantitative difficulty.

Within-item length spread: **tight at Easy** (median spread 0 words — options are one-slot
variants of a single template), **deliberately loose at Hard** (median spread 8 words). The key
is never systematically the longest option; across the bank the key is longest in only 13 of 32
literary items and shortest in 4.

### Polarity — the most-missed calibration

**214 of 226 items are "support." Only 12 are "weaken"** (5%), and 9 of those 12 are Hard;
**zero are Easy**. Publishers write far too many weaken items. Author **5 weaken items per 100**,
4 Hard and 1 Medium. Weaken stems use *weaken* (7), *undermine*, *challenge*, *call into
question*; never "which choice does NOT support."

### Answer key

Official spread: C 65 · A 60 · D 54 · B 46. **Author to exactly 25/25/25/25 per 100.** Never
three of the same letter consecutively in emitted file order.

### Rationale length

Mean **276 words**. Easy 183–200 · Medium 199–220 · Hard 238–280.

## 2. Family A — Quantitative (49 of 100)

### 2.1 The four stems

| Stem | Share | Skews |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Blank-completion** — *Which choice most effectively uses data from the {table\|graph} to complete the {statement\|text\|example}?* | 66 / 111 | Easy |
| **C. Describe-that-support** — *Which choice best describes data {from\|in} the {table\|graph} that support the {researchers'\|Nakamura and colleagues'} conclusion?* | 33 / 111 | **Hard (21 of 33)** |
| **B. Use-to-support** — *Which choice most effectively uses data from the {table\|graph} to {support\|justify} the underlined claim?* | 9 / 111 | flat |
| **D. Rare variants** | 3 / 111 | — |

The functional difference is load-bearing. **A** makes the option a *grammatical continuation of
the text's own sentence*: the student satisfies a local slot constraint (right row, right unit,
right rhetorical function). **C** detaches the option into a free-standing sentence about the
display, forcing a two-place check — *(i) is this description accurate? (ii) does an accurate
description of this particular slice license the conclusion?* Only **C** admits the distractor
that is perfectly accurate and cites only one arm of a two-arm comparison. That is why C is the
hard stem. **Author C-heavy at Hard and A-heavy at Easy.**

### 2.2 The difficulty ladder — what the text adds to the data

| | Easy | Medium | Hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) text is a frame; answer is a **direct lookup** | **27 (75%)** | 5 (18%) | **0 (0%)** |
| (b) text supplies a **rule/threshold** to combine with the data | 3 | 5 | 15 (32%) |
| (c) text states a **conclusion**; pick the data that supports it | 6 | 11 | **30 (64%)** |
| (d) text states a **contrast**; filter the data for it | 0 | 7 | 2 |

**No hard quantitative item is a lookup.** Hard items either hand the student a rule to execute
against the display ("higher ratios correspond to greater capacity"; "values further from 0
indicate a stronger relationship"; "this measure inversely correlates with oxygenation") or hand
them a conclusion and require the exact evidential shape that licenses it. The "contrast filter"
structure (d) is the Medium signature: *"most sites showed annual increases, but that trend
wasn't universal. For example, ______"*.

### 2.3 The trap shift — measured

Collapsing 330 wrong options into *misread the data* (wrong cell / phantom value / reversed
trend) vs *read the data correctly but attached the wrong conclusion*:

| | misread | wrong-logic |
|---|---|---|
| Easy | **83%** | 17% |
| Medium | 49% | 51% |
| Hard | 48% | **52%** |
| — by stem: blank-completion | 72% | 28% |
| — by stem: describe-that-support | 35% | **65%** |

**This is the whole game.** An easy quantitative item punishes sloppy chart reading. A hard one
punishes sloppy claim reading — the numbers in the wrong options are *correct*. Any authored
hard item whose distractors are wrong numbers is mis-levelled.

### 2.4 Distractor families (quantitative)

Every wrong option must be exactly one of these. Counts are from the official 330.

1. **Wrong cell** (151). Right kind of value, wrong row / column / series / time point. The
   engine of Easy. Official rationale form: *"according to the table, shrimp cocktail was served
   on day 4, not day 1; moreover, the item was served for meal C, not meal B, as this choice
   claims."* Always relocate the wrong value — say where it actually came from.
2. **Accurate but irrelevant** (72). True of the display, but about the wrong group, period, or
   variable for this claim. The dominant Hard mechanism.
3. **Accurate but one-sided** (44). True, on-topic, but cites only one arm of a comparison the
   claim requires. *"This accurately describes some data from the graph, but it doesn't weaken
   the hypothesis. It doesn't include the 'announcement after election' data for comparison."*
4. **Phantom** (25). A value, category, or variable not in the display at all. Hold near 8%;
   it never becomes the Hard mechanism.
5. **Reversed** (21). The comparison or trend stated backwards.
6. **Overreach** (7). Extrapolates, aggregates, or infers causation beyond the data. *"the table
   presents data only about people who chose to make resolutions … it therefore can't support a
   conclusion about US adults overall."* Hard only.
7. **Accurate but opposes the claim** (6).
8. **Fails a criterion the text stated** (4).

### 2.5 Data-object construction rules

**Tables (author 29 of 49).** One categorical label column + 1–4 data columns.
**Never exceed 5 data rows or 5 columns** — no official table does. Measured shape distribution:
3 columns ×22, 4 ×21, 5 ×12, 2 ×7; 5 rows ×24, 4 ×22, 3 ×11, 2 ×5. Units live in the **title or
the column header**, not repeated in cells (except `%` and `$`). Non-numeric cells are allowed
and used: `yes` / `no` / `N/A` / `not detected` / `no data available`.

Titles are long and fully specify population + measure + scope + date, with the unit in a
parenthetical:

> "Average Prices Received by US Growers for Citrus Fruits, 2020–2021 (dollars per box)"
> "Number of Museum Visits (in Millions) from 2016 to 2019"
> "E-book Sales as a Percentage of Total Unit Sales in All Book Formats for a Large US Trade
> Publisher, by Genre, 2006, 2011, 2016"

**Graphs (author 20 of 49).** Official mix: grouped/stacked bar 26 · line 14 · single-series bar
9. **Zero scatter plots. Zero error bars. Zero trend lines.** 2–3 series (max 4); 2–7 bar
categories or 3–11 line time points. Legends lowercase and prose-like: *"announcement before
election / announcement after election"*. Y-axis titles short with the unit in parentheses:
*"Survival rate (% alive)"*, *"Radial growth (centimeters per year)"*. When a scale needs
interpreting, say so on the axis: *"Rating (0 = no contribution, 5 = high contribution)"*.

**Number discipline.** Numbers are realistic and lightly rounded; significant figures are held
**per column, not per table**. **Arithmetic is essentially never required** — exactly one item in
226 needs interpolation. Where a derived quantity is needed, College Board **pre-computes it as
its own column** ("Inhabitants per guild", "Percent increase in area covered from 2009 to 2018").
Author the same way: rank, threshold, and direction comparisons only.

### 2.6 The blank lead-ins (a fingerprint)

71 of 111 quantitative items end in `______`, always with the stem on the next line. Verb
immediately before the blank, counted: *notes that* 6 · *concluded that* 6 · *found that* 5 ·
*finds that* 4 · *for example* 4 · *for instance* 2 · *noting that* 2 · *determined that* 2 ·
*showed that* 2 · plus singletons *citing as evidence the fact that*, *cites as a typical example*,
*as evidenced by the fact that*, *which helps explain why*, *was surprised to discover that*,
*given that*.

39 of 70 blank items have options beginning **lowercase**, continuing the stem sentence
mid-clause. Non-blank items always have capitalized standalone-sentence options. Never mix.

## 3. Family B — Textual: Finding (25 of 100)

### 3.1 Stimulus architecture — six templates

| Template | Share | Skeleton |
|---|---|---|
| **Research report** | 27/62 | *[Background/definition]. [Title + Full Name] used [method] to determine [finding]. The researchers hypothesize that [causal extension].* |
| **Design-withheld** | 10/62 | *…[A] and [B] hypothesized that [cause]. To test their hypothesis, the scientists did an experiment. They placed some [subjects] in [condition 1] and other [subjects] in [condition 2]. Then, the scientists watched [DV].* |
| **Anomaly → explanation** | 9/62 | *Although [group] generally [X], a subset [Y] instead. [Anomaly], despite [controlled factor]. Researchers hypothesize that this difference may be an adaptation to [cause].* |
| **Received view → challenge** | 8/62 | *Scientists have long believed that [X]. But [Name] and her team analyzed [data] and found [Y].* |
| **Lay claimant** | 5/62 | *[Figure] was the first [X] to [Y]. A scholar asserts that one of [figure]'s likely aims was to [purpose].* |
| **Rival scholars** | 3/62 | *Scholars such as [A] claimed [X]. Scholars such as [B], by contrast, argued that while [concession], [not-X].* Hard only. |

The attributive verb (*hypothesize / claim / conclude / argue / assert / posit / predict*) sits in
the **final sentence in 38 of 62 items**. The claim is either terminal, or underlined, but never
both. **10 of 62 use an underlined claim** — reserved for claims with no personal claimant, or
for compound claims where only half is at issue.

**Attribution style.** Full personal names 28 · "Name and colleagues" 12 · "Name and her team" 10
· generic "researchers" 5 · "a student" 3 · "a scholar" 3. **21 of 62 introduce the researcher
with a bare disciplinary title in apposition** — *"biologist Angela Stöger"*, *"Transportation
researcher Vikash V. Gayah"*, *"Plant ecologists Anna Abrahão and Patricia de Britto Costa."*
Reproduce this; it is a strong CB fingerprint. Tense: background present, study simple past,
claim present or present perfect.

### 3.2 The predicted-differential rule

The key is not a supporting fact. It is **the specific result the claim's mechanism predicts,
stated at the same grain as the claim.**

- **28 of 62 keys are explicit two-arm contrasts** — *"X did A, but Y did B."*
- **For design-withheld items the rule is absolute: 10 of 10 keys are two-arm contrasts.** If
  the stimulus names two experimental conditions, the key must report **both**. An option that
  reports only one arm is always wrong, and the rationale says so: *"provides no comparison with
  narrow-headed fish."*
- The other 34 keys are **single mechanism-bridging facts** — they insert the missing middle
  term, never a free-floating fact.
- Hard escalates to **difference-of-differences**: *"participants judged jewelry spaced far apart
  to be substantially more valuable at Tiffany & Co., but only slightly more valuable at
  Forever 21."*
- All four options share one syntactic frame per item: same subject noun phrase, same verb, same
  measurement unit. New proper nouns appear in **all four options or none**.

### 3.3 Distractor families (finding)

1. **Inert background** (54). True, on-topic, silent on the mechanism. The Easy engine (52% of
   Easy wrong options).
2. **Wrong variable** (47). Measures a different attribute than the claim — perception instead of
   knowledge, baseline instead of percent change.
3. **Reversed direction** (36). Would weaken a support claim. *"would strengthen, not weaken."*
4. **Broken differential** (28). One arm only, within-group instead of between-group, or the
   wrong pair. **Zero at Easy** — this is what makes Medium and Hard hard.
5. **Wrong population / period / place** (18).
6. **Half-claim** (3). Attacks the non-underlined conjunct of a compound claim.

Hard-only sub-mechanisms: difference-of-differences mismatch; **rival-mechanism undercut** (an
option that makes the proposed mechanism unnecessary rather than false); one-prong-without-the-
connective (establishes both facts but never links them).

### 3.4 Difficulty levers (measured)

| | Easy | Medium | Hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stimulus words | 75 | 91 | 101 |
| Words per sentence | 18.1 | 26.0 | **29.9** |
| Sentences | 4.3 | 3.8 | 3.6 |
| In-line glosses (parenthetical / em-dash definitions) | **0** | 9 | **34** |
| Competitor distractors as % of wrong options | 10% | 35% | 39% |
| Items with ≥1 real competitor | 14% | 53% | **74%** |

Easy = short declarative sentences, **no glosses**, one variable, three obviously off-topic
distractors. Hard = compressed multi-clause sentences with parenthetical glosses, an
**operational proxy** the key must be read through ("emotionality score", "percent change in
heart rate", "fusion and informativity as positive indices of grammatical complexity"), 2–3
tracked entities plus a control, and a two-step inference chain. Structural devices confined to
Medium/Hard: double negation, claim-about-a-claim, discrepancy-explanation stems (*"would best
account for the discrepancy [Name] observed"*), and compound conditional stems (*"Assuming
participants had similar baseline fitness levels, … had the predicted psychological effect but
not the predicted physical effect"*).

### 3.5 Weaken mechanics

Two key types: **(a) reverse the predicted differential** (6 of 10), and **(b) supply a rival
cause or dissolve the evidence** (4 of 10 — Hard only; e.g. *"the fossils are so poorly preserved
that they cannot be conclusively identified as ctenophores either"*).

**The characteristic trap is the mirror-strengthener**: 6 of 30 weaken distractors would
*strengthen* the claim. The second trap is the option the rationale labels genuinely neutral —
*"the finding would neither weaken nor strengthen the claim."*

## 4. Family C — Textual: Quotation (26 of 100)

### 4.1 Literary flavour (author 16) — exact template

> `⟨TITLE⟩ is a/an ⟨YEAR⟩ ⟨GENRE⟩ by ⟨AUTHOR⟩[, ⟨one-clause author gloss⟩][, originally written in ⟨LANGUAGE⟩].` `[0–2 sentences of plot setup.]` `In the ⟨novel/story/poem/play⟩, ⟨CLAIM⟩⟨: | , writing, | , saying, | , as is evident when ⟨X⟩ says⟩ ______`
> `Which quotation from ⟨TITLE⟩ most effectively illustrates the claim?`

- Sentence 1 is **invariant**. Titles italic for book-length works, curly quotes for
  stories/poems/essays.
- **All literary items end in a blank.** 100% correlation.
- **Plot setup is rationed: 20 of 32 give none.** Add setup only when the claim is unintelligible
  without it.
- Attribution tail: bare colon 20 · speech tail 12. The speech tail is **mandatory when the key
  is dialogue** rather than narration.
- Claim verbs, counted: *suggests that* 4 · *describes* 4 · *portrays X as* 3 · *presents X as* 3
  · *conveys* 2 · *expresses* 2 · then singletons (*depicts, emphasizes, predicts, praises,
  imagines, intimates, highlights, contrasts X with Y*).
- **What makes a claim writable**: it names one *psychological or rhetorical stance* attributable
  to a named agent (character / speaker / narrator / author), pitched one notch above the text's
  own words — never a plot fact. The claim's predicate must be **absent as vocabulary from all
  four options**, and checkable in a single sentence of the key.

### 4.2 Sourced flavour (author 10) — exact template

> `⟨1–3 sentences of neutral topic exposition⟩. In a ⟨paper/essay/article⟩, ⟨CLAIMANT⟩ claims that ⟨CLAIM⟩.`
> `Which quotation from ⟨SOURCE⟩ would best support the ⟨claimant⟩'s claim?`

**No blank — 0 of 21.** Claimants: a student 9 · a researcher 5 · the text's own voice 3 ·
historians 2 · a curator 1 · an arts journalist 1. Option sources: a work by a historian 3 ·
first-person voices (participant, survey respondent, shopper, platform user) 6 · an article about
X 4 · critics 2 · scholarly article / scholar / art historian / dance historian 4.

**Scoping so exactly one quotation matches**: the claim always contains a **causal or attributive
connector** that the three distractors sever. The claim names two anchors plus the relation
between them; each distractor keeps the anchors and drops or reverses the relation.

### 4.3 Option-set construction

**Literary.** All four options come from the **same scene, same character, same register** and
differ only in what they report — poems use four stanza-like passages, novels four narratorial
sentences about the same figure. Within-item length CV 0.16–0.25. **The key is not reliably the
longest** — make it shortest in roughly one item in eight. Distractors are made claim-irrelevant
by reporting a *neighbouring* fact: physical action, setting, a second character, a different
trait.

**Sourced.** Parallelism is tighter (CV 0.13). All four share one grammatical frame and name the
same proper nouns; register is held constant (academic third person *or* colloquial first person,
never mixed inside an item).

### 4.4 Distractor families (quotation)

| Type | Share |
|---|---|
| **Right subject, wrong attribute** — same character/topic, different trait | **30%** |
| **Half-claim** — satisfies one conjunct only | 23% |
| **Topical but claim-irrelevant context** | 18% |
| **Situation, not response** — action or setting instead of inner state | 9% |
| **Reversed direction** | 9% |
| Wrong referent / wrong time frame / effect-not-cause | 11% |

Two mechanical tricks recur and should be reproduced: **lexical bait** (a distractor contains the
claim's key adjective but applies it to the wrong object), and **cross-claim reuse** (the same
work can carry two items with different claims, where the key of one is a distractor of the
other).

### 4.5 The compound-claim trap

**19 of 53 claims (36%) are two-part** — *"both extremely virtuous and unforgiving"*, *"aware of
the intensity of her grief but believes it to be justified"*, *"acknowledges its merits while
also expressing displeasure"*, *"were aware of how materials affect sound and that they
deliberately applied this knowledge."*

Exploitation is systematic: when the claim is compound, **all three distractors typically satisfy
the same single conjunct**, and the rationale repeats one sentence three times. Half-claim
distractors are 3% of Easy but **29% of Medium and 31% of Hard**. This is the primary difficulty
lever in this family.

### 4.6 Difficulty levers

| | Easy | Medium | Hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Literary stimulus words | 31 | 37 | 39 |
| Sourced stimulus words | 48 | 74 | **95** |
| Mean option words | 23 | 25 | **35** |
| Half-claim distractors | 3% | 29% | 31% |
| Concessive rebuttals ("Although the quotation…") | 18% | 28% | 31% |
| Literary share | 85% | 54% | 50% |

**Easy** claims name one observable, emotionally legible state ("very upset", "nearly silent",
"predicts future success") and no distractor is partially defensible. **Medium** adds one
abstraction layer ("mixed feelings", "an element of nature as an active participant") and one
half-right distractor. **Hard** does four things at once: (a) abstract or meta-level claims
("ambivalence", "a style so idiosyncratic that it resists efforts to view his work through the
lens of nationality"); (b) archaic or inverted syntax; (c) figurative language that *must* be
decoded, not merely noticed; (d) two or three partially defensible options.

## 5. Rationale voice (mirror the official register exactly)

Two registers coexist in the official bank. Author **75% long / 25% terse**, with terse
concentrated in Easy quantitative items.

**Long register** — opens with the formula, restates the claim verbatim, applies the conditional,
then rebuts each distractor with a named mechanism:

> Choice **{L}** is the best answer because it presents a finding that, if true, would most
> directly support {claimant}'s {claim/hypothesis} that {claim restated verbatim from the text}.
> The text explains that {2–4 sentence paraphrase}. If it were true that {option restated}, that
> would suggest {inference}, thereby supporting {claim}.
> Choice {L2} is incorrect because {option restated} wouldn't {support / address / reveal anything
> about} {claim restated}. …

**Terse register:**

> Choice **{L}** is the best answer. {1–3 plain sentences.}
> Choice {L2} is incorrect. This choice doesn't support the claim. {One-clause reason.}

Non-negotiable features observed across all 226 rationales:

- Opens literally with `Choice {Letter} is the best answer`.
- **Restates the claim verbatim** from the stimulus before evaluating anything.
- Key rationales for quotation items **quote fragments of the winning option back** (40 of 53 do,
  mean 1.9 fragments), bracketing pronouns for grammar.
- **Misread rebuttals always relocate the wrong value**: *"el Castillo is 40 meters tall, not 33
  meters, which is the height of the Great Pyramid in Mexico."*
- **Irrelevance rebuttals always open with an explicit concession of accuracy** before pivoting:
  *"Although this choice accurately describes data in the table, …"* / *"This accurately
  describes some data from the graph, but it doesn't …"* This concession/relocation split is the
  single clearest tell of an authentic CB rationale.
- Compound claims get a two-column proof in the key rationale, one clause per conjunct.
- Never "is less precise", "sounds awkward", "is not the best choice".

## 7. Hard prohibitions

- No topic, person, organism, place, artwork, dataset, study or phrasing reused from the official
  bank export, official Practice Tests, or this repo's Practice Tests 3–5. Checked against
  `avoid_terms.txt` (1,350 proper nouns extracted from the export) programmatically.
- **Literary sources are invented, not real.** The official bank quotes real public-domain works;
  we do not, for two reasons. (1) Quoting 64 passages from memory guarantees misquotation, which
  is a factual defect in a paid product. (2) The repo's own `wic-refresh-2026` set already
  established the invented-source precedent (*"The Long Field," an 1893 story by Clara
  Weatherall*). Invent an author and a title, and write the passages in **genuine period
  register** — the item is indistinguishable to a student and cannot be factually wrong.
  Match the official source profile: ~40% poems, 20% plays, 20% stories, 13% novels, 7%
  nonfiction; ~57% US; ~43% women authors; ~33% writers of colour; ~20% presented as works in
  translation (flagged in-stem, as the official bank flags Sophocles, Tagore, Martí). Years
  1766–1925. **Never invent a work attributed to a real author**, and never reuse any of the 30
  works in the official export (*O Pioneers!*, *Treasure Island*, "The Bet", "The Yellow
  Wallpaper", *An Ideal Husband*, *King Lear*, *Electra*, *The Post Office*, *Hedda Gabler*,
  *Richard II*, *Happy House*, *Just Patty*, *The Souls of Black Folk*, and the twelve poems
  listed in `avoid_terms.txt`).
- No real living people; no real named studies; all researchers and their findings invented.
- No item solvable without the text or the display; no item with two defensible answers.
- No "NOT" stems, no all/none of the above, no lettered prefixes inside option strings.
- No arithmetic required beyond comparison — pre-compute any derived quantity as its own column.
