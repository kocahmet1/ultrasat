Q1 Words in Context easy key C 55 words
The following text is adapted from Adela Trenholm's 1921 story "The Salt Line."

Letters came into the shipping office from Alexandria and Odessa, and Odile copied them without pausing over the names. Her employer praised her speed. Yet the ports themselves stayed ______ to her: she could not have said what any of them looked like.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
A. crowded
B. familiar
C. abstract ✓
D. profitable
Choice C is the best answer because "abstract" most logically completes the text's description of how the ports strike Odile. The text establishes that the names of distant ports pass through her hands constantly: letters arrive "from Alexandria and Odessa," and she copies them "without pausing over the names," quickly enough that her employer praises her speed. The colon then supplies the sense the blank must carry, since she "could not have said what any of them looked like." For Odile the ports exist only as words on a page, with no concrete picture attached to them, and something known only as an idea in this way is abstract. Choice A is incorrect. Nothing in the text describes conditions at the ports, and Odile's inability to picture them tells us nothing about how many ships or people they hold. Choice B is incorrect because "familiar" reverses the relationship the sentence sets up. The names are familiar to Odile, but the word "Yet" marks the ports as different from the names, and a place someone can't picture at all isn't a familiar one. Choice D is incorrect. The text doesn't mention what the shipping office earns or whether the trade passing through these ports pays. Odile's work is described in terms of speed rather than profit, and calling the ports profitable would leave the statement after the colon unexplained.

Devised in 1928 by the poet Halldor Aasen, the braided stanza borrows the sestina's trick of repeating end words. Reviewing the first collection in the form, the critic Lucia Ferreira called it a dead end. She reached that judgment without hesitation, she wrote, yet she meant it to be ______ — a ruling in force only until other poets had attempted the form.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
A. equivocal
B. provisional ✓
C. tentative
D. categorical
Choice B is the best answer because "provisional" most logically completes the text's discussion of Ferreira's verdict on the braided stanza. The phrase after the dash states exactly what Ferreira intended her judgment to be: "a ruling in force only until other poets had attempted the form." Two features of that description matter. The verdict is in force, so it is a settled judgment that Ferreira expects to be applied to the form as things stand; and it is in force only until a stated condition is met, so it is meant to be superseded when that happens. A judgment that operates now and is understood to hold only until something replaces it is a provisional judgment, and the word therefore sits comfortably beside the report that Ferreira reached it "without hesitation," since it describes the standing of the verdict rather than the confidence with which she formed it. Choice A is incorrect because an equivocal judgment is one whose meaning can be read in more than one way, and Ferreira's meaning is reported as perfectly plain: she "called it a dead end." Nothing in the text suggests that her readers had any difficulty telling what she thought of the form. Choice C is incorrect because a tentative judgment is one its author has not settled and holds only unsurely, and the text rules that reading out directly. Ferreira "reached that judgment without hesitation," and a verdict she describes as being in force is one she is prepared to have applied, not one she is still weighing. What she leaves open is the verdict's future, not her present confidence in it. Choice D is incorrect because a categorical verdict admits no qualification and no later revision, whereas Ferreira attaches a condition to hers. She expects the ruling to lapse as soon as other poets take up the form, which is the opposite of stating it categorically.

Choice D is the best answer because it most accurately states the main purpose of the text. The first half of the excerpt establishes a settled routine and Elspeth's complete command of it: for twelve years "the columns had always come out even by four o'clock," and she "could set down a figure without reading it twice."
